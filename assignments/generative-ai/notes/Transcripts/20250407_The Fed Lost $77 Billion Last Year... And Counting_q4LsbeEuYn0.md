# The Fed Lost $77 Billion Last Year... And Counting

The [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is losing tens of billions of dollars every single year. And for the bank to be [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profitable]] and to stay in business long term, the amount that it makes from lending out your money needs to be greater than the amount that they pay to you forgetting to borrow your money. The [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] operates in much the same way and you can see the amount of money that they currently have loaned out by looking at their balance sheet.

Yes, this represents assets that they have purchased, but these assets are debt instruments, which means they do get paid back at some point. the assets that they hold on their balance sheet here are US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and mortgagebacked securities. Even so, they still have over $6.5 trillion worth of assets on their balance sheet. But it is also true that they also operate like a regular bank in that they receive deposits. It's just that they don't receive deposits from people like you and me.

And in return for taking those deposits, just like a regular bank, the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] does pay interest to banks. This is called the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]]. And it is the amount that banks get paid for holding reserves overnight.

So when people talk about the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] raising or cutting [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], it's specifically the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]] that they are talking about because that is the one [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] actually controls. And you can see how when the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] is very high, that would incentivize banks to keep their reserves with the Fed so that they get a really high [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] risk-free. However, if the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] is really low, let's say at zero, then those banks need to make a [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] on their money. So, they're going to push that capital out into the private market, try and lend it to individuals and businesses so that they can have a positive return on their money.

So paying interest to banks is one of the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] costs, but they have other costs as well. In fact, they are the largest employer of PhD economists there is to do something that literally a computer program could do and probably even more effectively. And finally, the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] pays dividends to its banks.

So, we talked about how the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] makes income, and that is from all of the interest they're receiving on all of the $6.7 trillion worth of assets that they hold.
