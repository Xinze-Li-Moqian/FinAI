# The New Bank Deregulation Plan Has Been Unveiled

The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just unveiled part one of their plan to deregulate the banks. They published a press release on March 19th detailing their plan along with hundreds of pages of detailed notes on this proposal. This deregulation would result in relaxed capital requirements for banks. They could unleash billions of dollars for lending, share buybacks, and dividends.

So, I'm going to explain to you in plain English exactly what this proposal for deregulation entails, why they are doing it, and most importantly, as always, what impact this could have on the overall economy and your portfolio so that you can protect yourself and [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] from it. If this is your first time here, my name is Joe Brown. I was a stock broker for years before I decided to start my company Harborside Financial, where I'm making money simple by making videos like this for you so that you can make money more simply yourself.

The first thing to take note of here is that this is not an immediate deregulation move. This is a package of proposals, and there's a 90-day public consultation before they can be finalized. That is why they released all of these notes so that they can receive comments. So, regardless of any of the impacts from these, there's not going to be any immediate action whether in the economy or in the overall markets.

It's going to take time. Looking through their summary, we can see there are basically three main parts to this proposal for deregulation, and the first one is going to apply to the largest systemically important banks. These are the GSIBs, globally systemically important banks, GSIB. This is going to be your banks like Chase, Bank of America, Citi, or etc. The summary states that the first proposal for deregulation would improve the capital framework by enhancing risk sensitivity, reducing burden, and improving consistency across banks.

They would be no longer using two sets of calculations in order to determine compliance with risk-based capital requirements. They'd only be using one. So, in plain English, what does this mean? Number one, these big banks would be required to hold less capital compared to their assets.

Now, for a bank, you have to remember that a deposit is a liability, and anything that they buy, or in other words, loan their money out for, that to them is considered an asset. After the Great Financial Crisis, there were a bunch of regulations set on banks in order to restrict them from doing things that might put them at risk of taking down the global economy. That's why some banks got the designation of a G-SIB, a globally systemically important bank, because if that one bank fails, it could take down the entire global economy along with it.

So, a bunch of rules and guardrails and regulations and laws were set up in place about what banks could lend money for, how much money they could lend on them, and what they had to keep in reserves or their capital in order to provide a buffer zone just in case there were losses. However, some of these regulations are competing against each other, and they are opposed to each other. And so, proposal number one here would be to simplify the way that banks calculate their risk limits, calculate their capital requirements, and this would also have the effect of effectively reducing their capital requirements, which has the other effect of allowing them to buy more assets, or loan more money out.

Now, in order to make sure that this doesn't allow banks to go out there and just be way more risky than they were before, they're going to be making a change to the way that banks calculate things from the entire year, rather than just at the end of quarter or end of year. I don't know if you've heard of window dressing, but at the end of every quarter, and especially at the end of the year, there are always spikes in usage of repo facilities, where banks need to go get a ton of cash because they need to make their balance sheet look pristine right when it's being looked at.

And so, instead of just looking at the end of the quarter or the end of the day, they're going to be averaging these numbers over the course of the entire year. This will make it a little bit more clear what real risks these banks are taking on over the course of the entire year, and it will allow them to lend a little bit more. Second proposal is going to apply to all banks except the G-SIBs, and it would better align capital requirements for traditional lending activities with risk while maintaining the framework's simplicity.

Consistent with the first proposal, it will reduce disincentives for [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] lending by modifying capital requirements for servicing and originating [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]]. It would also apply to banks that apply the community bank leverage ratio framework. It would also require certain banks to reflect unrealized gains and losses on certain securities in their capital levels. So, again, we have two things going on here.

One means that banks are going to have to report unrealized gains and losses on certain assets that they hold, which they're not required to right now. It's going to increase visibility into the real risks that banks are holding. And then again, the big change here is going to make it easier for banks to do [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] lending. This is going to apply to all banks except the biggest ones.

And then the third proposal improves how [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Systemic Risk|systemic risk]] is measured in the framework for determining the additional capital requirement for the largest and most complex banks. This will result in community and regional banks getting slightly less restrictive capital requirements, again, just like the first two proposals. Make it a little bit easier for banks to do everyday lending to small medium-sized businesses and individuals and increase overall lending. Okay, so those are the details here.

In summary, all of these proposed changes are very different than the supplementary leverage ratio that I've been discussing for months now. The supplementary leverage ratio is a separate restriction and requirement set on banks that forces them to purchase [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] and then penalizes them for doing so. Every single one of these changes here that we've talked about today will have no effect on the Treasury market. In fact, one of the effects of these regulations is that it may in fact actually increase Treasury yields slightly.

And this is because of this rule right here, banks needing to reflect unrealized gains and losses on certain securities. Reflecting and reporting unrealized losses in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] may have the effect of restricting banks' abilities to purchase [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], in other words, loan money to the government. So, this could very well have the effect of lowering [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on T-bills, the short end of the curve, but raising rates on the long end of the curve, on the things like 10, 20, 30-year, that are already struggling to find demand. And so, that's why I said this is part one of the overall deregulation plan that regulators have for banks, because this is targeting mostly what banks can and cannot do for the private economy.

This is loosening the standards there. It's probably this alone, if this is the only thing that happens, it's not going to have really any good impact at all, maybe slightly negative impact on [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], which is why we are still later probably going to see the supplementary leverage ratio either reduced or eliminated, because that will unleash the banks' balance sheets for unlimited Treasury buying while not hitting their risk limits to do all of this stuff that we're talking about here, increasing lending to the private economy. So, just to recap what I just said there, everything that is in this proposal here is about loans to the private economy, reducing capital requirements that will increase the amount of lending banks can do for things like [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]], business loans, [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]], credit cards, things like that.

But, this is separate from the supplementary leverage ratio, and it will have probably a little bit of a negative effect on long-term [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]]. Okay, so now, what impact will this have on [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] across the board? Very likely, these proposed changes will have moderate, small, very, very little effects in the short term, and just increase lending over the long term. Like I said, there's 90 days just for comment here.

This is multiple years here that we're going to see this take for it to unfold with greater lending into the overall economy. So, even though we are going to see restrictions lifted that penalize [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] servicing and originating [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]], it's not like we're going to see a boom in [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] originations. The main constraint there is [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are too high. There's just not enough demand for buying houses right now because [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are so high that monthly payment is unaffordable for most Americans.

So, even if it does knock down [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgage Interest Rates|mortgage rates]] by 25 basis points or even 50 basis points, that's going to do nothing for overall [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] demand and overall [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] affordability. We probably need to see 100 or 200 basis points. That's 1 or 2% lower [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgage Interest Rates|mortgage rates]] for it to have any impact at all on the overall housing market. So, just loosening the requirements a little bit that makes it a little bit easier to service and originate [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] is not going to do much to move the housing market.

It's not going to make [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgage Interest Rates|mortgage rates]] that much cheaper. And the same effect can be predicted for business loans, credit card loans, [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]], personal loans because we will see more supply across all of those forms of debt. Banks are going to be more easily able to make those loans and so that will drive [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] slightly lower. But, by itself, none of these proposed changes are going to lead to an immediate acute boom in lending.

Now, I mentioned before that the supplementary leverage ratio is also being considered to be removed. And if that happens, then that paired with these changes would absolutely result in a pretty sizable credit boom and a pretty sizable decrease in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] across the board for [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and all other forms of debt. But, unless and until we also see that SLR get removed from banks, don't expect the proposed changes here as of March 19th to have any major immediate impact on being able to refinance your debt for much cheaper or a huge credit boom resulting in massive economic expansion.

Now, the other reason why you should not expect a massive boom in lending immediately from this is because it'll also increase banks' abilities to do share buybacks and dividends. And so, if this extra lending capacity allows banks to offer loans marginally cheaper and that doesn't increase demand for borrowing that much, they may take that extra wiggle room that they have and just decide to do some share buybacks or pay out some dividends instead. And so, instead of this deregulation contributing to broader economic growth, it's very possible that this deregulation just increases the profitability of banks themselves, further worsening the K-shaped economy that we've already seen form over the last couple of years.

As they say, "Fortune favors the 500." Now, the other thing you should not expect here is a massive boom in risk-taking by banks, specifically because pretty much all of these changes are paired with different ways to make risk more apparent, more transparent, and easier to control. So, likely, no massive reckless boom in crazy risk lending.
