# Are We Just Repeating the 1970’s Inflation?

Are we on track to see a repeat of the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] of the '7s? If you take a look at this chart from Apollo, you can see two separate lines. One of them is indexed to 1966 and shows [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] unfolding over the next 16 years. As you can see, it slowly rose, then fell, then rose quickly, then fell again, and then finally had one major top before the current disinflationary cycle of the last 40 years began.

But you'll also see another line that is indexed to 2014. And you can see that in terms of the shape of the curve, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] since 2014 has largely been following the same pattern, though not the same levels. And starting in 2021, as a result of all of the money printing that began in 2020, you can see [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] spiked again, following the same pattern, which was followed by [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] subsiding, getting us to today where we are now. Now, unless you've been living under a rock, you know that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just lowered [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] again.

Zooming in on the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] chart, this is despite the fact that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] has actually been ticking up recently and hasn't been below their 2% target since before 2020. And it seems as if they're just throwing in the towel, submitting to political pressure and saying, "Okay, we will lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]." This as well seems like it's following the same pattern that they followed in the 70s. So you can see leading up into 1970, they were raising [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and then they cut. When [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] took off again, they raised [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] again.

They thought they won, lowered rates, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] took off yet again. And so then they continued to raise rates until they finally kicked it in 1980. Coincidentally, right around that time, they also changed the way they were measuring [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. But pay no attention to the man behind the curtain.

We can clearly see from this chart a pattern of raising [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] to try and stop [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. They think they won, so they lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and that fuels [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] yet again, leading many to believe that the current uptick in [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] along with the recent cut in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] is going to do the exact same thing and it's going to cause the Fed to have to panic and raise rates even more later on. Now, like I mentioned before, the magnitude of the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] from the 70s and of today is obviously different, but the shapes, the patterns are very similar.

And given the fact that lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] allow the US government to borrow and spend more, and that that borrowing and spending more is causing new money to come into circulation, that means we will have more money chasing the same amount of goods and services. There's good reason to believe that the recent rate cuts will lead to the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] spiking yet again. But there's a problem with this and it's a problem that I don't think we are likely to see the Fed respond in the same way they did in the 70s. And the problem is the debt to GDP ratio.

You see in 1970 the total [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|federal debt]] to GDP ratio was only 34%. And despite the lowering and raising of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] that took place over the following years, by the time 1980 rolled around, the debt to GDP ratio is actually lower at just under 31%. Contrast that with where the government is today, where the debt to GDP ratio is 123%. Keep in mind, at the peak of World War II, the debt to GDP ratio only got to 121%.

And this time, there's no war that's stopping in order to cut expenses. This extremely high debt compared to the size of our economy means the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] can't just raise [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] if [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] starts to take off again. It would force the US government to go broke. The US government simply cannot afford to borrow at higher rates.

Which is why although I think the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] pattern of the 70s is likely to play out again, in other words, brace for impact over the next 5 to 10 years, prices are probably going up. I don't think we are likely to see the Fed respond in the same way with jacking up [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] in order to quell [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] like they did during the 70s. It is much more likely in my opinion that they do the same thing that they did after World War II that helped the US government delever and bring its debt to GDP ratio down.

Very simply, that is that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] helped the government inflate its debt away. The way they did this was by engaging in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control so that the US government could borrow money at a rate that was lower than the rate of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. In other words, negative [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Nominal and Real Interest Rates|real interest rates]]. This meant that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] was being transferred from all lenders to the government.

Now, if you're sitting there thinking, "Well, good thing I'm not a lender to the US government." Not so fast. You probably are and don't even know it. Number one, in any brokerage account or IRA or [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Retirement Accounts|401k]], if you have any sort of a balanced fund, a target date mutual fund, any sort of investment basket that is decided on anybody but you, I would put money on the fact that you probably have government [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] in there.

Unless you have specifically and intentionally picked out every single one of your investments and all of the baskets, all of the funds you've picked out to be 100% stock and equity funds and some of your nest egg is going to be lent to the US government. And by the way, even if you are in 100% US [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]], where do you think all large companies keep their cash balances? They keep their cash balances in banks, in tea bills, and in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. which means that if you are an owner of large corporations, some of your assets, some of your net worth is indirectly being lent to the US government.

On top of that, if you have any cash allocation in any of your investment accounts whatsoever, whether it's a brokerage account or an IRA, that cash behind the scenes is getting put into things like T bills. Again, it's being lent to the US government. If you have any money whatsoever in a checking account, in a savings account, a high yield savings account, a money market fund, guess what? All of these instruments are just middlemen.

They're just pass through entities. The way you're getting paid interest on that is that that cash is being lent to the US government. And so unfortunately in this day and age, it is basically impossible to not have some of your money exposed to being lent to the US government, which means you have to work all that much harder in order to make sure your income and your wealth keeps up with [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] because they're stealing some of it.
