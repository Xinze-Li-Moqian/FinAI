# Why Lower Inflation Means More Stimulus Ahead

[[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|Inflation]] fell again last month, more than expected, which means we're probably going to see that money printer fire up again soon. You can see this chart of the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] shows April year-over-year [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] fell to 2.3%. That means prices in April 2025 were 2.3% higher than they were April of 2024. Now, this was a drop from a month earlier in March where the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] was at 2.4%.

Now you can see even though it's been volatile for the last couple of years, the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] has been moving lower. Now some economists were astonished by this because they expected that the [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] would be pushing the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] up higher. That is not how [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] work. When you artificially make some things more expensive with a [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]], it just means that people have less money overall to spend everywhere else, which means [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] slow down the economy.

Without a broad-based increase in the overall money supply, you would not be getting a spike in prices. I don't know why this is such a hard concept for mainstream economists to grasp. So, why am I saying that this is likely to allow the money printer to fire up again soon? Well, it's because the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] by law is mandated to care about three things: employment, prices, and [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]].

Now, the thing that makes their job difficult is that they only have one tool to manage all three of these, and that is either money printing or [[Finance/15 Monetary History/Money Creation and Destruction|money destruction]], injecting liquidity, or withdrawing liquidity. When they're printing money, it will help [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]], but it will hurt prices. when they're destroying money or withdrawing liquidity, it will help prices, but it will hurt employment. And so, the fact that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is continuing to fall means that they don't have to worry about the negative effects of money printing pushing up prices.

And in the face of a softening labor market with rising initial jobless claims, they will have more freedom to lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|quantitative tightening]] or maybe even resume [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] again. Now, I mentioned before that they also care about [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], and this is the little known third mandate given to them by Congress, which is that their job is to moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. Now, whether that means 10-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], 20-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], or 30-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], that is left up to interpretation. And whether that means 3%, 5%, 10%, or somewhere in between, that is also left up to interpretation.

But it is a fact that it is their job to maintain moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. And that job has been given to them by Congress the same way that they are told that they have to care about maximum employment and stable prices. Now, earlier I talked about how [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]] and [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] moved opposite to each other when the Fed decides to print money or withdraw money from the system. So, how does that impact [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]?

Well, it actually depends on where we are in the long-term US debt cycle. The last time the US government was in a [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]] phase was from 1940 through 1980. During this time, you can see [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] rose significantly. During this same time period, the United States government's debt compared to the size of the GDP dropped from about 120% down to about 30%.

During this time, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] engaged in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control and other tools to help the US government delever. This caused higher [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], higher [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for the average person, but it allowed the government to perform an inflationary [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]]. Now, the pendulum swung back the opposite direction from around 1980 until 2020 with [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] dropping over time, allowing the government to relever up. You can see during that time, US debt to GDP rose from about 30% all the way back up to 120%.

Now we have entered the [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]] phase of the US debt cycle yet again with [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] rising, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] rising and the government's debt to GDP ratio starting to fall. This will be another inflationary [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]] where the US government gets to borrow at rates lower than the rate of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] helped by the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. This causes [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] for all of us which pushes [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] up for all of us. Even though there are certain periods of time you can see where [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] do fall, the overall trend is higher.

And the same will be true here. Since everything bottomed in 2020, we have and we will continue to see short-term periods of time where rates will fall. The long-term trend here is an inflationary [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]] for the US government. So if and when [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] do dip a little bit, those are times to take advantage.

Lock in low rate debt for as long as you can. aggressively buy assets that will be lifted from all of this money printing, from this inflationary [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]]. But understand that for many, many years, potentially decades, we will not see the same type of environment that many of us are familiar with. In other words, this 40-year time period of falling [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and falling [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is done.

It's history. That 40-year [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] bull market is over. We are in a [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] bare market now. And increasingly, the biggest buyer will be [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] like the Fed. Like I said, this is going to be very beneficial for asset prices, which is one of the reasons why we've already seen a new bull market emerge from the bottom of the bare market as investors start to price in that new future reality.
