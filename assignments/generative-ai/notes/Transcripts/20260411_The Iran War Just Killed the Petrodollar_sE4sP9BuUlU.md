In July of 1944, a group of nations met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire. This is where they established a plan for how the international monetary system would work following World War II. Now, this was necessary because during World War II, all the European nations had spent all their money. At that time, money was gold.

They had spent all their money fighting the war. And guess where all that gold went? That's right, the United States. So, the nations sat down together and came up with a plan that they would back up their money with the dollar because the dollar was as good as gold.

In fact, at that time, anybody who was not a US citizen could redeem their dollars for gold. And so, the plan was set, and over the course of the following decades, everybody on Earth was using dollars for their currency, and the United States got the advantage of being able to export dollars to the rest of the world in exchange for real goods. Now, the problem is these dollars that we were sending out around the world were not backed up one-for-one with gold. It was a fractional reserve system.

So, we were printing way more dollars and sending them out to buy stuff than there actually was gold to back it up. Fast forward to the end of the '60s, 1970-1971, the rest of the world was starting to figure this out. So, they were starting to redeem all their dollars for gold, and Fort Knox was being drained. In fact, if nothing would have happened, we were about 2 weeks away from all of the gold being completely gone.

And so, Nixon decided to close the gold window, hoping that it would be temporary, and hoping that things could just go back to normal, and everybody would stop requesting the gold. But, obviously, there is nothing as permanent as a temporary government plan, and the gold window stayed shut permanently. This meant that the United States had essentially confiscated the gold from the rest of the world because the rest of the world was promised, "Hey, at any time you can come get your gold." But, when they tried, we said no. And in fact, one of the last countries to get their gold was France because they actually sent a warship to get their gold from New York.

And so, in early August, they were able to collect their gold, and just a few days later on August 15th of 1971, is when Nixon shut down the gold window and ended dollar-gold convertibility. This officially ended the Bretton Woods system, and it put the dollar's role as the [[Concepts/Finance/International Finance/Reserve Currencies|global reserve currency]] under extreme threat. And that's where the petrodollar system comes in. A few years after the gold window closed, we get to 1974 when Henry Kissinger struck what some call one of the most consequential financial deals in modern history. That meant those dollars came right back to the United States government so that they could continue borrow at cheaper rates.
