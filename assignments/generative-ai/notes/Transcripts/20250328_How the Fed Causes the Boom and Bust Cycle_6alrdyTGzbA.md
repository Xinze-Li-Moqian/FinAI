It's not a [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Business Cycles|business cycle]] and it's not caused by the free market, overexuberance or irrational fear by market participants. It is a debt cycle caused by the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. And their manipulation of [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] is what causes booms and makes the following busts inevitable. In order to understand what's going on here, we have to talk about how money works.

First, in a free market, the easiest way to visualize what is going on here is to imagine a desert island with a closed economy where they use gold coins as money. There's a limited supply and if you want to get more money, you have to provide a product or a service to other people who will want that product or service so much that they're willing to give up some of their money for it. In other words, there is no player who has the ability to just arbitrarily create more money for themselves, giving themselves [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] that other people don't have.

In a free market economy like this, sometimes the savings pool will be full. In other words, people have lots of savings because they haven't been spending, they haven't been investing, and they haven't been borrowing, they've simply been working and putting money away. In an environment like this, if I went out to the market with some of my money and tried to loan it to somebody, because here the savings pool is full, everybody has been doing the same thing as me. So, there's really no demand for the money.

Everybody has money for themselves. That means if I want to entice somebody to borrow from me, I will have to offer them a very low [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]]. In other words, in a free economy, when the savings pool is full, it naturally pushes [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] down because money is abundant relative to goods and services. You can see how low [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] in this environment would be a signal to somebody that they should start spending and they should start borrowing and they should start investing.

In an environment like this, people would naturally understand everybody's got a lot of money. And so if I borrow a little bit of money at a very very low [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] and use that for productive purposes, then I can make a return that is way larger than the amount that I'm going to have to pay on the debt. And so a large savings pool will naturally incentivize spending, borrowing, and investing. And it does this through the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] being naturally pushed down.

That is a signal sent to economic participants that it's time to spend and grow. But if this process goes on for too long, you can see how the savings pool would start to dwindle. Everybody starts to borrow. Everybody turns into borrowers. There would be very few people left with savings to lend out.

In that situation, the person with the savings will be able to demand a much higher [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] because everybody is a borrower and a spender. So, they're all competing for the little bit of savings that is left. This means there is now a [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] opportunity just to have money because you get to be the lender. In other words, the high [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] which are caused by the scarcity of the money supply, the low savings pool are a natural incentive to start saving again and to stop spending and to rebuild that savings pool.

In this system, [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] would eb and flow would constantly be changing obviously depending on the borrower, the project, what the money is being spent on, but it would keep a natural equilibrium between the supply and the demand for money and would make sure that there weren't any booms and busts because as the savings pool dwindles down, the incentive to save grows at an equal pace.
