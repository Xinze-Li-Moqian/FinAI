# The Great Rotation Just Started

Great rotation may have just started between small cap companies and large cap companies in the US stock market. Take a look at this chart which shows the relative performance of the Russell 2000, which is small caps, versus the S&P 500, which is large caps. And you can see that for over a decade, small caps have been trending down relative to large caps. However, it looks like that trend has recently ended and small caps have been outperforming for the last few quarters.

And recently have just broken back above the 200-day simple moving average for the first time since 2021. If this is not just a short temporary bounce, the implications for the economy could be significant, especially what this means for markets going forward for the next 5 to 10 years plus. The reason is because the last time you saw small caps begin to significantly outperform large caps was right around 2000. And you can see that relative outperformances lasted years.

This long-term cycle between relative performance between large and small cap companies lasts usually anywhere between 5 to 10 years. That's what it did after the dot-com bubble burst, where you saw small caps outperform for about 5 or 6 years. And then, you had an extended period of roughly the same performance until about 2018 when things really took a nose dive for small caps and they've been heading down ever since. Which means we are right on time for a new rotation back into small caps.

Now, if you take a look at just the chart for the Russell 2000, you can see that for years the Russell 2000 was basically moving sideways. That was during a time where the S&P 500 was in a very strong bull market. The Russell 2000 was just moving sideways. However, recently it has broken out and it has sustained a very strong bull market.

And just year-to-date, the Russell 2000 is already up 20%. While the S&P 500 is also up year-to-date, it's only up 9% right now. So, we've seen double the performance out of the Russell 2000, out of small caps, so far this year compared to large caps like the S&P 500. Now, this massive difference in performance can only happen from big money flows, from institutional changes in how investments are being allocated.

Which means while everybody is paying attention to SpaceX and Micron semiconductors and memory [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]], big money is positioning for a much larger cycle in small American companies. And this is despite the fact that 40% of Russell 2000 companies earn nothing right now. On top of that, consensus earnings forecasts for the Russell 2000 fell 7% in the first 5 months of this year through May 18th. Over that same time period, S&P 500 earnings forecasts rose 8%.

So, what in the world is driving so much institutional money to flow and rotate into small caps compared to large caps when it doesn't look that great for that. Well, one important reason is valuations. You've been hearing everybody and their mother-in-law talk about how overvalued the stock market looks like right now. That's large caps.

It's the S&P 500. That's tech [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]]. That's not the Russell 2000. It's not small caps. First of all, over the long term, price-wise, small caps and large caps tend to have very similar performance. You have a couple of years back and forth where they will swing, one or the other will outperform versus the other.

And in fact, here's another chart of what that relative outperformance and underperformance looks like between small and large caps going all the way back to 1936. But from a valuation perspective, right now the Russell 2000 looks extremely cheap. You look at the price relative to sales or revenue for the companies inside these indexes. You see the S&P 500 price-to-sales ratio is near an all-time high, where the Russell 2000 is nowhere near the S&P 500.

You can also take a look at valuations in these indexes based on the price-to-book value. And while again, the S&P 500's price-to-book ratio is near all-time highs, very close to where it was at the top of the dot-com bubble. You can see the Russell 2000 has been roughly flat over that same time period, maybe even trending a little bit down. Even just looking at the dividend yield of the companies inside these indexes, you can see that Russell 2000 small caps is much more attractive than large caps right now.

So, from strictly a valuation standpoint, small caps do look much more attractive than large caps currently. So, that is one thing that could be driving institutional flows to rotate from large to small right now. But, that's not the whole story because there's usually a good reason for lower valuations, and it's simply that the market expects that that company will not do as well in the future. Valuation is always just a game of asking, "How much should I be paying for a money printer?"

And if you expect a money printer is not going to do that great at printing more money for you in the future, you're going to spend less money on that money printer. That's all valuation is. That's what you're trying to do when you're assigning a value to a company, seeing if it's undervalued or overvalued, seeing if you're paying too much or a good amount to get your hands on a money printer. And if most participants in the market are saying, "I don't really want to pay a lot for that money printer," there's probably a good reason.

However, from a valuation perspective, small caps do look much more attractive than large caps right now. So, valuation is not the big reason, then what is? If the recent outperformance of small caps compared to large caps is truly the start of the next leg of this long cycle of relative performance between small and large, and we're about to see another huge rally in small caps relative to large caps, just like we saw after the dot-com bubble burst, then the reason is likely more to do with [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. A couple of days ago, I posted a YouTube video on my channel explaining why I think that [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are going to be headed lower, not higher, despite the fact that the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just came out and said hey, we are going to crush [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]].

They're not going to do it by raising [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. This expectation of lower [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] coming up is one of the reasons why so much money has been driving small caps so much higher recently. But why? What would [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts have to do with small caps and why would it have more to do with small caps than large caps?

Well, large corporations typically issue fixed rate debt. This locks in their borrowing costs for years. However, smaller companies must rely on floating rate debt, which means their interest costs on their debt go up as the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] raises rates. In other words, a large company when they borrow money, they do it similar to how when you go get a [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] or maybe a [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Auto Loans|car loan]].

Typically, unless unless you're really like playing with fire, you're going to go get a fixed rate form of debt, meaning you know the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] is going to stay the same until you pay off the loan. However, back in the Great Financial Crisis when everybody was borrowing a bunch of money to buy a bunch of homes, most people's [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgages]] were variable. They were adjustable rate [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgages]], which meant if the Fed raised [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], then the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on those [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgages]] were going to go up and your payment was going to go up.

That is exactly how many small companies, which are part of the Russell 2000, have to borrow debt. They're not able to lock in a fixed rate for an extended period of time. And so as the Fed raises [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], their own borrowing costs go up in real time. And I told you earlier about 40% of the Russell 2000 companies do not generate earnings.

A big part of this has to do with their higher cost on debt. When they cannot cover those higher interest costs, they have to do things like sell more shares, draw down credit lines, or take other actions that hurt company performance. Which means that if I'm right and what looks like to be the bet of massive institutional money flows, if they are right, too, it means number one, we should expect the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to cut rates later this year, not raise rates. And that means that this breakout of small cap outperformance should be sustained because it significantly decreases the costs for many small cap companies, which means they get to increase their earnings, they get to decrease their costs, they have more [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profits]], and it directly impacts them way more than large companies.

And with everybody looking around right now at the SpaceX IPO and microchip [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]] just exploding, memory [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]] exploding, and saying that everything today looks just as bad as it did right before the dot-com bubble burst. Well, if we really are at beginning of another dot-com bubble burst, then small caps are really where you want to be. Now, a couple of warnings. Number one, over the long term, when small caps do outperform, usually that's the beginning of an economic downturn.

Now, I personally think that if the tailwind behind this is [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts, then that's going to be a tailwind behind the entire economy. But, it is a warning that you should be careful and look out for. Second thing is that relative outperformance is just relative. It doesn't mean absolute outperformance.

In other words, from 2000 through 2006, small caps drastically outperformed large caps. But, that just means they either went up more than large caps or they didn't go down as much. And you can see that from 2000 through 2003, small caps did actually head lower. They just didn't head as low as quickly as large caps.

So, keep in mind there's a difference between relative and absolute performance. However, if you have to choose where to put your money or where to put some of your money, then you would still outperform even if that just means lose less. Now, I'm not telling you to go out there and rebalance your whole portfolio or sell all the large caps and buy all the small caps. But, this is something significant to look out for the long term, not just for a short-term trade.

This is one of the reasons why I preach having uncorrelated assets. And the relative difference in performance between small and large cap indexes are one of the easiest ways to get uncorrelated performance in a regular stock portfolio. Let's say you have a hypothetical allocation of 20% small caps, 20% large caps, well, it'd be fairly simple to capture the outperformance and underperformance of each of those by simply rebalancing every quarter or every year between the two. Because during times where small caps outperform, it means that your small cap allocation would go from 20% to maybe 25 or maybe even 30% or more of your portfolio.

You rebalance, you're selling high and buying low relative to each other. And then on the way down when small caps underperform relative to large caps, you do the same thing. Your large caps end up being a large percentage of your portfolio, so you can sell and buy the other. This gives you the ability to increase the shares of both slices of your pie even if you're not contributing any extra money.

Given the fact that so much institutional flow is rotating into small caps, it's possible that it wouldn't be such a bad idea to add a little extra money. As always, thank you so much for watching. Have a great day.
