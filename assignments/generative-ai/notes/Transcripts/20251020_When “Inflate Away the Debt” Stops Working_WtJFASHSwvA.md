# When “Inflate Away the Debt” Stops Working

When in the course of human history, governments are staring down the barrel of a [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis, usually they try and solve this problem by inflating away the debt. And while that has worked in the past, even in the United States, I'm going to show you why it's not going to work this time. Right now, you are looking at a chart of the total debt that the federal government owes as a percentage of the size of our economy measured by GDP. You can see right now the debt to GDP ratio is over 120% and it has been that way for about 5 years now.

You can also see that the United States has been here before in 1946 as a result of the spending and borrowing for World War II. While having a very high debt to GDP ratio like 120% isn't the definition of a [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis, it is generally accepted by most economists that this is a level that is very difficult, if not impossible to come back from, which gives some people hope because the United States has been here before and has come back from it. While there's no firm definition for [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis, the one I like the most is when interest payments on the debt exceed total [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|tax]] revenue.

In that situation, a nation cannot pay down its debt because the only way it can pay any of its expenses is by borrowing. All of the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|tax]] dollars are already getting soaked up just to pay the interest on the debt. It's worth noting we are not there yet. The United States collected about $5 trillion in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] this last fiscal year.

Yet, it only spent just under $1 trillion on the net interest cost of the debt. If you include the total interest cost on the debt, it is higher than one trillion. But some of that goes to other places inside the government. So they look at the net interest.

As I'll show you, if nothing changes, we are headed to that point where total [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|tax]] revenue will be surpassed by the interest on the debt. But again, we are not there yet. So again, that's giving people hope there's still time to come back from this, especially if we can just inflate away the debt. The reason why that's the playbook is because that's what they did last time to come down from World War II.

Some simple [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control and [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Financial Repression|financial repression]] meant the US government was able to borrow, issue [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]] at an [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that was below the rate of [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]]. The entire economy really had no choice but to absorb this loss themselves. But that's only looking at one side of the coin. We have to take a look at everything else that was happening during that time. Mainly the spending.

There was an insane spike from about 10% meaning the size of the economy. they were spending. The government was spending about 10% of GDP. Had an insane spike to over 40% of GDP and then very quickly crashed back down to just under 11% of GDP.

This highlights very clearly that it wasn't just the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control and the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Financial Repression|financial repression]] and trying to inflate the debt away that allowed the government to escape its [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis.
