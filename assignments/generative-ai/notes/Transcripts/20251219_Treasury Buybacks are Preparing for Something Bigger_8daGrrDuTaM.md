# Treasury Buybacks are Preparing for Something Bigger

The US Treasury keeps on increasing its buybacks and everybody is confused as to the real reason why. In fact, they've been increasing their buybacks so much lately that they have recently hit a record with how much debt they're buying back. But these buybacks have a lot less to do with what is happening right now and a lot more to do with what they are preparing for in the near future. So first, what even is a Treasury buyback?

The United States Treasury Department is the department of US government that handles the finances. So take money in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] or when they borrow money, that all comes through to the Treasury. And then whenever the government spends money, whether it's on a social security paycheck, Medicare, government contractors, politicians' paychecks, it goes out through the Treasury. We also know that the US government spends a lot more every year than they take in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]], which means the difference is borrowed.

They do this borrowing by issuing debt, which just means borrowing money, and they sell things called [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. So they sell an IOU worth, let's say, $1,000, which means they receive $1,000. And in the future, they pay back, let's say, $1,050. That piece of paper, that IOU, can be traded between individuals.

Whoever owns that piece of paper is going to be the one who receives the $1,050 from the US government at some point in the future. Okay, so that's normal. What is a buyback then? Well, it's when the Treasury itself goes out there and buys that IOU from the open market.

Now, you might think, well, that doesn't make any sense cuz then they'd owe money back to themselves. And really, what's happening is they're just paying back this debt early. This would be very similar to you doing a credit card balance transfer. You have a balance on one credit card, you open up a new credit card, and you just transfer the balance from one to the other.

You are using new debt to pay off the old debt. And specifically in the case of the US government, that is exactly what they are doing here. They're using new debt to pay off old debt because, like I said a moment ago, they always spend more than they take in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]]. So the difference is made up through borrowing, which means if they're going to be spending even more by paying off some of that old debt early, retiring that old debt, buying back some of those [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]], they're only able to do so by borrowing even more from somebody else.

It is very, very simply a balance transfer. And now I'm going to show you a few charts that you'll need to understand as we talk about the next part here. These are the yields or the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on current US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]], depending on how long it is until that debt matures. So this first chart that we're looking at is the six-month [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury bond]] or [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury bill]].

Currently has a yield of 3.6%. This is an annualized number, which means if you get this thing twice in a row for six months at a time, at the end of the year, you will earn 3.6% from loaning your money to the government. The one-year currently has a yield of 3.5%, which means, yes, you're right, the US government right now can borrow for one year at a cheaper rate than they can for borrowing at six months. And actually, that extends even farther if you look at the two-year yield right now, it's at 3.48%, which again is just slightly cheaper, which means the US government can borrow for two years at a time, they don't have to pay that debt back for two years, and that is going to cost them less in total interest than borrowing for one month or six months at a time.

But that's where the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curve Inversion|inverted yield curve]] ends. When we go and look at the 10-year yield, we see it's all the way up at 4.15%, which is significantly higher than the short-term debt. In fact, it gets even higher at the 20-year yield, which is at 4.78% right now, and it's highest at the 30-year at 4.826%. This is called a steep [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]], which is a normal [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]].

It basically means that if the government borrows for longer periods of time before they pay that money back, they're going to be charged a higher [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]]. And that's normal. If you borrow a 30-year [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]], you'll get a higher [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] than a 15-year [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] because there's more time for stuff to go wrong. There's more time for [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] to spike unexpectedly in that amount of time.

So lenders want a higher [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] for longer-term debt. Why does this matter for the buybacks though? Well, we can go to the Treasury's website, treasurydirect.gov, to see what they are buying back. And you can see on December 18th, they were buying back the 20-year to 30-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]].

On December 17th, they were buying back the three-year to five-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. On December 4th, they were buying the 10-year to 20-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. On November 25th, they were buying the one- to 10-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. And on November 20th, they were buying the 20- to 30-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]].

Now, you'll notice I skipped a couple in there. The ones that I skipped were the short-term ones that they were buying. Again, anytime you hear buyback, if that doesn't click with you, just think paying off early. And again, the only way they're paying it off early is by borrowing other debt from somebody else.

The reason this matters is because the long-term debt, like 20- 30-year, five-year, 10- to 20-year, 20- to 30-year, this is all the most expensive debt. That's where the yields are highest right now. But you'll notice that just a couple of years ago, they were nowhere near as high as they are right now. And the US government did borrow some at the long end of the curve when [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] were down at half a percent and 1%.

So current reduction of interest cost is not the only reason they are doing this right now. It's not even the primary reason they're doing this right now. You'll notice there's this column on their website that shows the operation type. And every single one of these buybacks, that is the longer-term maturity debt that they're buying back, has liquidity support labeled as the reason.

Now, this is important. This signals that there is just not a ton of demand for the long-term debt compared to the US government's appetite for borrowing. US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] are supposed to be the most liquid and deepest markets in the world, and yet the US government themselves has to go in and buy back its own debt to make sure that any sellers of that long-term have somebody to sell it to. Liquidity support is a sign of weakness and a sign of thin trading in those markets.

And the most obvious reason why is because [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]] over the next 10, 20, 30 years are higher than what those yields are paying right now. If the market overall believed that [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] over the next 30 years was very probably going to be 2% or 3%, there probably wouldn't be that much of a problem lenders to lend to the US government at close to 5%. But given the deficit, given the rate of borrowing, given the rate of spending increases, and everything else going on in the economy, lenders are just not lining up to loan the government money for 30 years.

That means when somebody wants to sell that debt, remember I talked about earlier, it's like an IOU that people can sell, and whoever owns that IOU is who gets paid back from the government. Whenever somebody wants to sell those [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]], sell those IOUs, there's not enough depth in those markets to absorb all that selling. That's why the US government is coming in to support that liquidity, to be the buyer of that debt to make sure prices don't plummet. And the way they are funding this, like I have mentioned a few times, is by borrowing again from somebody else, and they're doing this at the short end.

I made a video a couple of days ago about how $9 trillion of debt needs to get paid back this year, and that's because so much of the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] has been concentrated in the short end. The US government is borrowing primarily through T-bills. That's debt that matures at the very short end of the curve, within a year. And so they're borrowing extra of debt that matures very soon in order to pay off some more of that debt that matures over a longer period of time.

So remember the example I gave earlier where I said that what they're doing is like a credit card balance transfer? That's close, but not as close as we can get. Because we know that they are borrowing at the short term in order to pay off the long term, this is more closely related to if you were taking out debt from a credit card to pay off your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] early. Your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] doesn't have to be repaid for 20, 25, 30 years, but you are taking out debt on your credit card in order to make extra payments on your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]].

Now, this might sound ridiculous and highly risky, but it's because you're not in control of rates, or at least you don't have a large amount of influence over [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. Whereas the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], in conjunction with the federal government, they have a lot more influence over [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. And so if you have a [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] that has a rate of 5%, but you have an introductory rate on your credit card for the first year at 3.5%, it might make sense to you to take out extra debt on your credit card, use that to make extra payments towards your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] because you are expecting at some point in the near future, [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgage Interest Rates|mortgage rates]] themselves will come down to, let's say, 3.5% or even lower.

And at that point, then you will refinance your entire [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] to pay off your old [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]], but also to absorb and repay off all that credit card debt you built up along the way. So right now, you're building up credit card debt to shrink your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] debt cuz the plan in the future to bring back up your [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] debt to get rid of your credit card debt. And the only reason this plan is working, at least so far, is because there's such high demand for T-bills right now. T-bills are the debt that mature in under a year, and there is just a massive amount of demand for that.

Every dollar that's in a high-yield savings account or a money market fund, or even just cash inside of brokerage accounts or IRAs, all of that behind the scenes, or most of it, I should say, is being swept into T-bills. And that's because you know you're going to get paid back within a manner of four weeks, six months, a year. So you really don't have to worry that much about a big spike in [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] that would happen in the short term. You also don't really have to worry that much about what other assets will do because it's such a short amount of time.

You're not locking your money up for 10 years, 20 years, 30 years at a time. So, just from the market right now, there is massive demand for that short-term US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]], so they're taking advantage of it and they're borrowing as much as they can at 3.6% or 3.5% or 3.48%. Now, there's also, just started recently, extra demand for T-bills because now the Fed themselves are actually buying T-bills. I made a few videos about this as well, but on their balance sheet, they're still letting mortgage-backed securities drain off, but as that money gets repaid back to them, they're rolling that money over into T-bills.

So, they're actively building up their holdings of T-bills just from that alone. But, that would be leaving their balance sheet steady. In addition to that, they're also buying T-bills and short-term debt up to 3 years of maturity, which means they are increasing their balance sheet again. This is [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] light or not [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] because they say the purpose of this is to make sure [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]] stay where they want it, but it doesn't matter.

Either way, they're growing their balance sheet, creating new money into existence, and buying US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]], loaning those dollars to the US government, and it's at the short end of the curve. The Fed is buying the six months, the one years, the two years. And so, the US government themselves can borrow at that end of the range a lot more than they would be able to otherwise cuz a new lender has just entered the scene. So, between the Fed and the overall market, there's a lot of demand for short-term US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] right now, which is allowing them to shrink the overall average weighted duration of the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] by paying off some of that long-term and squishing it toward the short term.

But, all of this is for nothing if they can't eventually roll it back over and refinance into longer-term debt. Otherwise, they have all of their debt that's constantly maturing every couple of months or every year. And then, if [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] go up, man, those interest expenses just explode. So, at some point, the plan is we're shrinking it down here so it's all available and constantly rolling over so that then we can take advantage of long-term rates going down.

And that is the real plan. That is the thing that Bessant has been talking about since this administration got into power. That is the entire goal is getting long-term [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], at least the 10-year, down. And that has not happened yet. Overall, the 10-year yield is at the same place that it was all the way back in October of 2022.

It has been volatile, it has moved up and down since then, but has remained range-bound, which means that likely the only way they get long-term [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] to go down and stay down enough with enough demand for them to roll over and refinance the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] to longer-term duration is with some form of [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] or [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control. This is the 1940s playbook. Ever since 2020, we entered into phase two of the long-term debt cycle again, just like we started in 1940, it ended in 1980. Phase one then started in 1980 again, and now we are back in phase two.

That is when the debt-to-GDP ratio gets too high and the government decides they need to deleverage, forcing the cost onto the economy. Last time they deleveraged, they did so through [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] and [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control. So, [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] went up, but the Fed made sure that the government could borrow at a rate less than the rate of [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]], which meant prices were going up, [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] were going up for everybody, but the government was able to deleverage through [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]]. Now, in addition to the Fed possibly doing something like [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control, we are very likely to see [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] start through the banks.

There's been a lot of talk about deregulating the banks so that the banks can buy an unlimited number of US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] without risk. Right now, banks are limited in how many [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] they can buy due to their risk limits called the supplementary leverage ratio. With that gone, it frees up the banks to just buy as many US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] as possible, and because they're loaning money into existence, they don't care about the actual [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]], they just care about the spread they can make on the money they're borrowing versus the money they are lending.

And if you have your money in a bank account, you are the lender to the bank, so they borrow your money, and then they lend it to US government, and they keep the difference. And that could absolutely push the 10-year, 20-year, and the 30-year much, much lower, unleashing trillions of dollars to be loaned to the US government.
