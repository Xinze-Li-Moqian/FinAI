# Markets are About to Do the Unexpected

Investor sentiment has rarely been as low as it is right now. Despite the fact that the stock market is basically back up to all-time highs, the fear and greed index is still reading extreme fear territories. And it's not for no reason. There are plenty of reasons to be pessimistic about the economy and the stock market right now.

Everything from fears about an AI bubble about ready to crash to the fact that the jobs market is starting to look very bleak with this being the first year with multiple negative months since 2020 and the fact that delinquencies on debt from student loans to credit cards to [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]] are spiking. Many people rationally are fearful about the near future financially speaking. With that in mind, is there any reason to be bullish? Is there any data that makes the outlook optimistic?

Because it's all fine and good to just say be greedy when others are fearful, but what if the people are fearful correctly? Because it's very easy to say be greedy when others are fearful. But if you're anything like me, you probably want a little bit of data going into that decision if you're going to act on it. So, the first thing that you probably haven't seen in the news lately is that retail sales have increased about 4% over the past year.

And that's despite the fact that consumer sentiment has dropped off a cliff during that period of time. This is why consumer sentiment or even a retail investor sentiment for that matter is often considered to be a contrarian indicator because people are the most fearful or the most greedy at the absolute bottoms or the absolute tops when in fact the data showing what people are actually doing is showing that people are actually spending more. But often times individual behavior can be financially uh reckless. Let's just say for the least.

It's not just individuals though. It's not just retail sales. When we look at spending by large corporations, we see capex spending from tech and tech related [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] is off the charts right now. And again, many people might look at this as a sign of a bubble.

But typically, if a market is at the top of a bubble, you don't see Berkshire Hathaway taking a stake in one of the companies that is doing some of the most spending in that area. More often than not, Berkshire Hathway taking a large stake in a company near the beginning of big bull markets, not at the end. But it's not just investor behavior. If we take a look at [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]], we now see that [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts by the Fed are now priced in at an 87% chance of happening at the next meeting.

We're moving forward into an environment that is largely an easy money environment. For the past couple of years, things have been tight ever since they unleashed all that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] from all the money printing. For a couple of years, they were trying to pull back on that. They didn't really do the best job, but they were raising [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]].

They were lowering their balance sheet and they did drain quite a bit of liquidity out of the system. That has come to an end now, come to a screeching halt. They have pivoted. They have reversed course. They're cutting rates and ending the tightening phase.

Looking at the implementation notes from the last FOMC statement, we see that beginning on December 1st, [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] will roll over at auction all principal payments from the Fed's holdings of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury securities]], which means any [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] that get paid back to the Fed, Fed will take that cash and just go out to the market and buy more [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. They won't let them bleed off their balance sheet anymore. But it doesn't stop there because again starting on December 1st, they will also be reinvesting all principal payments from the Fed holdings of agency [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities into [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury bills]].

In other words, all the [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities as they get paid off and bleed off the balance sheet of the Fed, they're going to take all that extra cash and also roll that into T bills. This is taking liquidity from one area of the market, [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities, and injecting it into the broader economy overall through decreasing the cost of government borrowing. And therefore, the government can spend more money, print more money into existence easier. And it's not just the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] doing this.

[[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|Central banks]] all around the world have recently stopped raising rates or started cutting. So from a [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] standpoint around the world, we are entering into an easing cycle where liquidity is increasing. And if you've wondered why gold has skyrocketed like 100% over the last 2 years or so, it's because it fronts [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]]. Or more accurately, it's because the people who are buying gold, driving the price higher and higher, know that they are about to change [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]].

[laughter]

Next up on the list, we have [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Wages|wages]]. Now, there was a period of time where [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Nominal and Real Wages|real wage]] growth was steeply negative, which is to be expected. This is how the canon effect works. When new money gets printed, first it gets paid and goes to the hands of whoever is closest to the money printer.

So, it goes to the government first, then it goes to the politically connected. As that money gets spent the first couple of times from one person's bank account to the next, prices have not yet had the time to adjust. they haven't had the increased demand from the increased amount of money in order to get bit up. But as that money gets spent through the economy, filters its way down into the paychecks and the [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Wages|wages]] of individuals, prices have now gotten bit up.

Which means by the time people get raises and get those [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Wages|wage]] increases, prices have already run away from them. And so when you adjust for that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Nominal and Real Wages|real wage]] growth is negative. However, for 29 months now, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Nominal and Real Wages|real wage]] growth has been positive. Now, again, here you have to trust that the official [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] numbers they're using to adjust [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Nominal and Real Wages|nominal wages]] are accurate.

And that is very easy to contest, and I'm with you on that. But what is also true is that if you compare what's going on now to what was going on before, at least it is much better. So, it's possible, maybe even probable, that [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Wages|wages]] are still not quite keeping up with [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], but they're getting a lot closer now than they were a couple of years ago. Now, even though [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Wages|wage]] growth has been increasing, a lot of people are still concerned that everybody is still losing jobs.

The problem with that is that initial jobless claims are actually moving down. They're at their lowest level right now since August, which means if everybody was actually getting laid off right now, this number would be moving up, not down. Now, the next big piece that a lot of people are mostly not going to look at is corporate [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] sales, which so far in 2025 just hit a new record at about 6 trillion. It may not be apparent on the surface why that would be a bullish thing for the economy or for markets, but what are corporations going to do with money that they borrow?

They're going to spend it. And corporations do not spend money the way that individuals do. Yeah, you might have some executives paying a little bit more for some private flights or first class upgrades or a renovation in the corner office suite, but for the most part, corporations are looking for a positive [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Investment Returns|ROI]] on their money. And when they borrow, they have to pay interest on that, which means they're planning to spend that money in a way that will make them more than what they owe.

That is the entire point of running a business. It's called [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]].

>> [laughter]

>> And that's what businesses try and do. Which means when you see corporate borrowing skyrocketing and hitting new records, it also means corporate spending on things that are planned to make more money is also going to go up. This means things like spending on R&D, on hiring, on building new factories, buying new properties, increasing production. These are signs that you see coming out of a [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Recessions|recession]] or coming out of a difficult economic time.

These are not things that you tend to see right at the beginning or at the top. At the beginning or at a top is when you start to see everybody pull back on spending, hunkering down for the hard times. And so while it may seem that everything is about ready to implode and collapse, it might just be your cognitive bias, looking for evidence that matches what you think is true. I'm not making the claim here that everything is beautiful and hunky dory and bury your head in the sand and just pretend everything is going to be okay.

I'm presenting evidence that maybe the near financial future might not actually look like a catastrophe. And here's the most important part. Markets climb a wall of worry. And so if you are expecting everything to implode and you have positioned yourself to only be able to [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] if you are right, you are setting yourself up for failure.

Because the mark of a good investor is not how many times you are right versus how many times you are wrong. It is all about how much you make when you are right versus how much you lose when you are wrong. So look at the evidence and invest according to what is happening, not what you think should be happening. That is at least as long as you actually want to make money, which at the end of the day is actually the goal of investing.
