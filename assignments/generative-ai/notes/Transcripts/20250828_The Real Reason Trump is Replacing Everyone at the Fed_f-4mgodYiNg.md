# The Real Reason Trump is Replacing Everyone at the Fed

A radical reshaping of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is taking place right now with a complete overhaul of its leadership and who is making decisions about [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]]. But the big question is why? Why does the Trump administration want new people appointed so badly? I'll give you a hint.

It is not just about [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts. Although that is what has been talked about mainly, I think the real reason has a lot more to do with $3 trillion at the Fed that Trump wants unleashed to buy [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. And I'm going to show you how new leadership in place there could accomplish this goal. Now, most people are aware that ever since Trump got back into office, he's been talking about figuring out ways to get rid of Powell.

Even looking at the cost of renovating [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] buildings as a way to justify firing him. Seems like that reasoning did not look sufficient enough to hold up in court, which means he may just need to wait until he appoints Powell's successor. But it's not just Powell that Trump is replacing because on August 1st, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board announced that Adriana Cougler would step down from her position as a governor of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board effective August 8th. Now, this resignation was given without cause, without reason, but it opens up a spot for Trump to appoint somebody else to take Cougler's place, but things are heating up because it looks like Trump is going to get another appointee on the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board because he just fired somebody else on the board.

With this executive order, President Trump referred to his authority under article 2 of the Constitution and the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Act of 1913 and said, "You are hereby removed from your position on the board of governors of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] effective immediately." The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Act provides that you may be removed at my discretion for cause 12USC 242. And I have determined that there is sufficient cause to remove you from your position. Now, the cause cited here is that Cook allegedly lied on [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] applications.

Within the course of a few weeks, a couple years ago, Cook applied for two [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] to buy two different houses in two different states, and she stated on both applications that that house would be her main primary residence. Now, I'm not sure whether this is confirmed. I don't know whether she has been charged with a crime yet at this point. She definitely hasn't been convicted.

But what is interesting is that while the president does have authority to fire [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board governors for cause, nothing states what that cause needs to be. Nothing defines what counts as cause or not. Clearly, lying on [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] applications is not something directly tied to her duties at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], which is why she has said that he has no authority to fire her. and she stated, "I will not resign."

Now, this whole thing is super interesting for a couple of reasons. Number one, a couple of weeks ago, we just got a no explanation resignation from Cougler coming out of nowhere. Maybe somebody talked to her behind the scenes and said, "Hey, look, we want you to resign or else." Maybe they've been doing digging on everybody at the Fed trying to figure out if there's any way they can push some old people out to put their own people in.

And maybe, just maybe, Cook said no. And so Trump tries to fire her anyway. Now, the second reason that this is interesting is because we don't actually know whether this will hold up in court. There's no definition of what the cause needs to be for the president to remove one of the governors from the board. However, this didn't really have anything to do with her duties at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]].

So, we don't know which way the court will rule here. And clearly, she's not going down without a fight. But this is also interesting for a third reason because it puts the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] itself in a very sticky situation. Because what if the court rules in Trump's favor and says, "Yep, he actually does have the authority to fire her, but the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] allows her to continue her duties this entire time."

Well, at that point then, it's possible that Trump could fire Powell for allowing somebody to continue operating at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], voting, making [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] decisions who didn't actually have the authority to be there that whole time. So the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] may itself just have to go along with this for fear that they might be held liable in case this goes in favor of Trump. Now this will be very interesting to see how all this unprecedented stuff plays out. But the question I asked at the beginning of the video is the important one here.

Why is this happening? Why is Trump so set on replacing people on the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board of Governors? Well, if he gets this push through, he's going to have a 4 to3 majority on the Fed's board of governors, meaning four of the people on the board will be appointed by him. Now, you might think, well, that means [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are going to do whatever Trump wants to do.

But not so fast, because the FOMC, who sets [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], is made up of 12 members, not just seven. Those 12 members are made up of the seven members of the board of governors of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] and the president of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Bank of New York and four of the remaining 11 reserve bank presidents who serve one-year terms on a rotating basis. In other words, the committee, the FOMC, who decides [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] is not just made up of the board of governors, those seven people. Those seven people Trump will have a majority appointees on, four out of seven.

But the other people who vote on [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] who make up the FOMC are five people who rotate presidents of these banks. And Trump does not pick who those people are. Now, it is true that the board of governors have veto power over who the reserve bank presidents are. Now, historically, this has never been used.

However, you could make the argument that because they all know that the board of governors ultimately could choose who they want to be the presidents that the people who get to that position to get voted on for reserve bank presidents are already going to be people that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board of Governors want. I know it's confusing, but basically you've got the board of governors that are appointed and confirmed by Congress and then they have voting power, veto power over who the reserve bank regional presidents are. All of them together, those 12 people together make up the FOMC and they vote on things like [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]].

And so having extra people that you want packed on to the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board of Governors does influence overall [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]]. But again, this isn't the big reason. The big reason comes down to interest on [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]] held at the Fed. Right now, there's $3.3 trillion that banks hold at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]].

These are [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]]. And what you can see from this chart is that banks did not keep reserves at the Fed until after the financial crisis. Following the financial crisis, the reserves that banks hold at the Fed have grown to the point where now about $3.3 trillion is sitting there. Now, this $3.3 trillion gets paid an [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]].

So, banks hold their reserves at the Fed and get paid to do so. Currently, this is the tool the Fed uses to make sure that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]], the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that they set, stays within their target range. It makes sure that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] never falls below their floor because if one bank tries to offer a lower rate to another bank, the other bank will just keep their reserves at the Fed instead. The interest paid on this $3.3 trillion is the floor underneath [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]].

However, it is not necessary for the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to use this in order to make sure the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]] stays within their target range, as is obvious because there were no balances held at the Fed prior to 2008. Prior to 2008, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] used open market operations to increase and decrease reserves in the system by buying and selling [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] in order to make sure that [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] stayed where they wanted them to be. Now, this is much harder. It is less efficient, but it is still possible.

Now, currently, I've talked about this before, but there is a bill proposed in Congress that would prohibit the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] from paying earnings on reserves held at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] from this point on. Essentially, this bill would make things go back to the way they were before when the Fed had to manage [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] through open market operations and they couldn't just set a floor by paying interest on the reserves that banks hold at the Fed. If banks can no longer get free interest from the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] by holding their reserves at the Fed, then banks have to do something else with their reserves by [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]].

And if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] stopped paying interest on overnight [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]], that's exactly what would happen. $3.3 trillion of [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]] would leave the Fed and would flood into US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], driving prices up and [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] down. But what does this all have to do with Trump reshaping the leadership at the Fed? Well, this bill requires Congress. It requires rewriting the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Act or at least making an amendment to it.

And that is unlikely. However, if you can appoint people at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] who will make changes that you want them to, maybe you can accomplish this goal without Congress. Especially considering that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Board of Governors alone controls [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] other than just the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]], including the interest paid on reserves that banks keep at the Fed. In other words, Trump's stacking the board in his favor might get [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] overall a little bit lower in his favor.

But that's not the big deal here. The big deal is that the board of governors alone without the regional presidents, they decide the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] on the reserves that banks hold at the Fed. The amount the Fed pays on this $3.3 trillion. Suddenly you have a 4 to3 majority on the board of governors and they can decide to decrease the rate paid on those [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]] to zero or significantly lower and all of that money rushes out into [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] instead.

It doesn't matter what the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] is at that point. It doesn't matter that a bill hasn't gone through Congress. You can effectively accomplish the same thing, moving the reserves out of the Fed into [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] simply by getting a majority on the board of governors who agree to do so. And you don't even need the whole entire FOMC, which includes the rotating regional presidents.

This is significant because if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just lowers rates like Trump says he wants, it is not likely to significantly lower rates of things like the 10, 20, and 30-year US Treasury. It will lower rates of tea bills, but that doesn't help the government roll over the $10 trillion of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] that matures every single year that they're just rolling over into more tea bills because they can't afford to rolled over into the longer term debt. But $3.3 trillion of new demand for [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] could at least in the short term significantly make a dent here.

Not to mention the fact that if the Fed stops paying so much interest on [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]], then the Fed suddenly becomes profitable again because they've got a large balance sheet and they're not paying out anymore on reserves, which means all that income that they're making gets swept back to the Treasury again like it used to. So, I think that is the real play here. Obviously, we're going to have to wait and see how this firing of Cook plays out, but within the next couple of months, we could see some major restructuring with who's in charge at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], major leanings towards rate cuts, and most importantly, a draining of the reserves held at the Fed to push that money back out into [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]].
