# Dollars ARE Debt

Most people don't realize this, but all money is debt. And the way policymakers manage that debt is why [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] is not only permanent, but it is about to come back with a vengeance. So, first let's talk about the concept of money and why money itself, regardless of the form, operates as a form of debt. Whatever is used as money by a country or an economy or society in any place in the world and in any time in history is simply the most saleable good.

In other words, it's the thing that you can exchange for other things most easily with the least amount of friction the most universally. If you imagine a desert island scenario where some of us are catching fish, some of us are getting coconuts off of a tree, we might very easily end up using coconuts as our form of money because coconuts are somewhat durable. You can store up on coconuts that you have gathered and then use them to make a trade for something else like fish and you can use them in the meantime.

But, they're not going to operate as money super effectively with a lot of people over a large amount of time because they rot. So, you can't store them for a long time before you need to use that. Also, it's fairly easy to go get your own coconuts which means you don't necessarily have to participate in any labor or provide any service in order to get that. This is why throughout history economies have settled on gold as the form of money that they choose to use.

Because of its physical properties, it doesn't rust, it doesn't decay, it's very difficult to counterfeit, it's easy to identify because of its color and its malleability and it's easy to divide into smaller and smaller pieces. These properties make it useful as money and so people generally started to accept it as payment just because they knew other people would accept it as payment. I might accept rice as payment, but then I'm limited to using that rice as payment to somebody else only if they're willing to accept it as payment. This is why money tends to become a winner take all game because the monetary usefulness of that good or that commodity tends to rise above all else.

Everyone ends up accepting it as payment just simply because they know everyone else will also. And so, I can store the value of my labor in that money as long as I want until I choose to exchange it for something of real wealth. In a very real sense, this turns money into a form of credit or debt, an IOU that I can use to exchange with anyone else in the economy to get something that I actually want. Money by itself is useless.

Its value is specifically in its use as money. Which means that when I work, when I sell my time, when I create a product or a service, and I freely exchange that with somebody else in the economy, and in return they give me money, whether it's gold or dollars or something else, I am temporarily storing the value of what I just created. And it operates like an IOU to society, an IOU from the entire economy that I can use and I can exchange at any time. It really is like a form of debt that I can say to the economy, you owe me a specific amount of wealth, and I can go cash in on that at any time.

And even before [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Commodity Money|commodity money]] like gold, there's good evidence that debt itself, credit, favors, operated as money before gold even did. This is documented in a book by David Graeber called Debt: The First 5,000 Years. People would record the debt that others owed to them on things called tally sticks, and then they would use those as money instead of him owing me a favor in the future, I'm going to use that as money and buy something from somebody else, and now that guy owes that person a favor. So, whether in the beginning when it was actual debt that was being traded as money, or gold being used as IOUs or a form of debt as money, or today when we sell our products and services and we get dollars and then we hold on to those dollars until we decide to cash them in and exchange them for real wealth, money is debt.

But it goes even further than that because the very foundation of the financial system today, which is the dollar, is real debt, actual debt, not just an IOU from the economy. When you get paid, your dollars get deposited into a bank account. At that point, the legal structure is that the bank owes you those dollars back. You are lending those dollars to the bank.

You are the creditor, they are the borrower, which means the bank then must go do something with your dollars if they don't want to go out of business. Otherwise, they would have to charge you to hold onto your dollars and keep them safe. But, they don't think that's a very good business model, and so they just take your dollars and go loan them out to somebody else so that they can make an [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] on your money. Sometimes they loan those dollars out to somebody who's going to be buying a house with a [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]], sometimes they loan them out to somebody who's using a credit card, sometimes for an [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Auto Loans|auto loan]], but often times, the vast majority of the time, they're just loaning your dollars to the US government in the form of buying a treasury.

But, either way, no matter what, you lend your dollars to the bank, and then they take your same dollars and loan them out to somebody else, usually the government. But, guess what? It doesn't stop there because once the government gets those dollars from the bank, they do something with those dollars, they spend that. And so, then the government spends that money into the bank account of a politician or military personnel or government contractor.

And as soon as those dollars hit the bank accounts of whoever received those payments from the government, the bank then takes those dollars and lends them out again. And this process continues over and over and over again where the same dollars are being lent from one bank account to another to another to another. This is called rehypothecation. It means that the same dollar is used to be loaned out over and over again, which means it's not actually really there.

Everybody owes dollars back to somebody else earlier in the chain. This is why [[Concepts/Finance/Banking and Credit/Bank Runs|bank runs]] can happen because if everybody at the bank actually tries to go get their dollars, they're not actually there. They look like they're there, and because of that, it looks like the money supply continues to grow. These dollars are not actually anywhere.

They're deposits in accounts, but if everybody tries to go get them, they're not there. They're not real. And the foundation [clears throat] of this entire system is the US Treasury debt that the US government borrows. So, what's the problem? The problem with this is that debt must be repaid.

Debt has a maturity date. But on top of that, all debt carries with it an [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]], that today, if they just stopped increasing the money supply, at some point in the future, the dollars that are in circulation would start to decrease. Because I have to pay back my [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]], I have to pay back my credit card debt, I have to pay back my [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Auto Loans|auto loan]] with dollars that are in excess of the dollars I borrowed. If I borrowed $100, I have to pay back $105.

Which means when that debt was created, $100 came into existence, but in the future, $105 have to leave circulation. And when debt gets paid off, the exact opposite happens than what happened when that debt was created. Dollars are lent into existence, and when debt gets paid off, those dollars cease to exist. Both the liability and the asset on both sides of the balance sheet disappear.

Which means that every dollar that is created causes temporary [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]], but plants into the system future [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]]. This is why in the past, we would have booms and busts. We have credit expansions, and then credit contractions. Because that's the natural thing that happens when you lend money into existence, and debt gets paid off, and the money ceases to exist.

But the powers that be don't like the contractions. They don't like the busts. Which means that this requires future printing. And when I say printing, I don't mean the Weimar Republic style of printing. Back in the day, when they would print money, they'd actually print money.

Paper dollars would come into existence. When you print paper money, you're not planting any future [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]]. You are simply increasing the money supply and that's it, which is why in the past [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Hyperinflation|hyperinflation]] could get out of control so quickly. But now when we see the money supply with electronic dollars that are being lent into existence, every single one of these dollars represents future demand for even more dollars, which means that left alone we would have defaults and we would have [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]].

Just like the Great Depression, but today much, much worse because there's way more debt, a much larger foundation of fake money that it's all built on. If they were to just to step aside and stop intervening, it would all rapidly disappear. Prices of everything, assets, gold, houses, cars, food would plummet because prices would have to adjust to the new real money supply, which would be much, much smaller than it is today. Now in reality, this wouldn't actually hurt anybody in real terms because it wouldn't affect the amount of production that's available in the economy.

It wouldn't affect the amount of labor. There's still the same amount of people who can work. There'd still be the same amount of food people can eat, the same amount of cars people can make and drive, the same houses that people can live in. So real tangible assets and [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Productivity|productivity]] would not be impacted.

It would, however, change the number you see on the screen. It would change the number you see in the bank account. It would change the number on the price tag. So everybody would feel a lot poorer and that is precisely what policy makers want to avoid, which means every time we get to a point where we're about to tip over into [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]], they come in and print what's necessary to stop it in its tracks.

Every time the US government gets to a point where it's borrowed so much there's no more money left in the economy for it to borrow, the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] comes in and creates more. Now it just so happens that the United States government has so much debt that it is worth 123% of the total US GDP. You can see that is over quadruple what it was just 40 years ago. However, we've been here before.

The last time the US government had this much debt was because of World War II. Now, when a government has this much debt compared to the size of the economy, it can't [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|tax]] its way out. There's just not enough money, not enough [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Productivity|productivity]], not enough growth of real wealth for them to confiscate. And so, if the government wants to deleverage itself compared to the size of the economy like it did after World War II, which it took 40 years to do, a large part of it has to come from inflating the debt away.

Remember, they can't borrow and [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|tax]] their way out of this. They have to create a bunch of new dollars. Flood the economy with them so that compared to the size of the economy, their debt load goes down. This is done through things like [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control, [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]], [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Financial Repression|financial repression]], and more.

All of these are tools that are starting to be implemented now and will continue to be implemented into the future. It means the government will be able to borrow at very low [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] from banks and from the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. But people like you and I and businesses will be forced to borrow at much higher rates. Why?

Because [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] will be high. If the government is borrowing and spending money into existence, that increases the money supply. That bids up prices. That means lenders don't want to get screwed on the lending, so they're going to charge a high [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] to compensate themselves for that [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]].

You and I are not the government, so we can't force lenders to lend to us at a rate less than the rate of [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]]. And so, prices and [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for the broader economy are going to be going higher over the next 5, 10 plus years, which means if you're expecting to be able to borrow at lower and lower rates like people have been able to for the last 40 years, you likely just haven't realized yet that ever since 2020, things are going the opposite direction. We're in a new phase of the long-term debt cycle.

And so, if you're still living and planning and investing as if we are in the last phase, things will likely get more and more difficult for you. Things like staying invested in uncorrelated assets, especially assets that tend to perform very well during times of high [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]], and most importantly, making sure your debt load stays extremely manageable and that you are not banking on the expectation that you'll be able to refinance at a lower rate in the future. These are all things that will probably keep you ahead of the curve. As always, thank you so much for watching.

Have a great day.
