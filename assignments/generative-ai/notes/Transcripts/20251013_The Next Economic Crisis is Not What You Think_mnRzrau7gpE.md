# The Next Economic Crisis is Not What You Think

The next major economic crisis is not happening to the banks. It's not happening to the stock market. It's not happening to corporations or taxpayers. It's happening to the government. It is a [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis. And we know this is the case simply by studying history.

Because every single time for the last 30 years that there has been a major crisis, the next crisis happens to whoever absorbed the one from before. And knowing the epicenter of the next crisis allows us to prepare for it and [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] from it. So let's go back 30 years approximately to the year 1998. There are a few guys running a hedge fund called Long-Term Capital Management.

These guys names were Mertton, Scholes, and Black. If those names sound familiar to you, it's because you're an options trader and the options pricing model was named after them. It's not because these guys were idiots. In fact, they are Nobel Prize winners in economics and they were running this hedge fund that was the envy of the entire banking world.

They were making money hand over fist with a new exotic strategy that could make tons of money with zero risk. And you know what happens when somebody is making a ton of money with a strategy that has zero risk? It means there's actually a lot of risk. It's just hidden somehow.

This fund was performing a strategy called relative value trades. It's a type of arbitrage trade. For really simple example of an arbitrage trade, you could look at the two share classes of Google stock, go and go L. You can see that over the long term, these two share classes perform identically to each other. This makes sense because they are both equal shares of the exact same company.

But when we zoom in, we can see that there are short periods of time where there is a small amount of outperformance or underperformance by one share class versus the other. And that also makes sense because different entities hold different amounts of these shares. And so when different entities have to dump or purchase large amounts of these shares, the price movement can change, which means that the short-term performance of the two share classes can be different. When this happens, it creates an arbitrage opportunity, which means you can buy the one that has underperformed and short the one that has outperformed.

By doing this, you are bringing in the exact amount of money needed in order to purchase the shares. So, your net outlay on this trade is zero. You also are price agnostic or performance agnostic for the company as a whole because both share classes could go down or both could go up and you don't care because your gains and your losses from the short and the long are going to offset each other. The only thing you're banking on is that the performance will eventually converge.

Once it converges, you close the trade out and you walk away with the tiny [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] in between the two. Now, obviously, you're going to be picking up a few cents here and a few cents there doing this strategy, which is not a good recipe to make money. So, you're going to have to use tons of leverage with this strategy. That way, your return on your capital will actually be worth it.

Mechanically, that is exactly what long-term capital management was doing. They were just doing it with currencies and [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] and more exotic instruments, but mechanically they were just finding things that had diverged that statistically would converge again. They just had to hold long enough until it converged to make the trade worth it. And that's where the problem came in because on paper, this strategy had zero risk.

But in 1998, it started to blow up because there was so much money riding on these trades that when it didn't start to converge when it should have and it diverged a little bit too much, that forced some traders out. That forced some money to close out their positions before it had converged, which made the situation worse. They're buying what they shorted and they're selling what they were long, which drives the divergence even further apart. that squeezed the rest of the market and you started to have traders all across the world get closed out and margin called and very quickly this was tipping over to the point where the entire global financial system was at risk of collapsing.

So the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] got together with a couple big banks and they said, "Hey, we need you to deal with this problem." The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] did no printing. They did no cuts. They just told the banks, "You better handle this." And the banks handled it.

They got together and they purchased and bailed out long-term capital management and wound it down. If you're interested in a really good book about this story, I can I highly recommend When Genius Failed by Roger Loenstein. Very good book on this topic. But that situation came and went and most people never even knew anything about it.

But it set the stage. It set the precedent. It planted the seed for future bailouts. And like I said before, the next crisis always happens to the entity that absorbed the last one. So in 1998, the banks bailed out a hedge fund. They swallowed that storm.

And in 2008, just 10 years later, they were the epicenter of the new crisis. Now, we don't need to go into as much detail about the great financial crisis because you lived through it. It was big news. Everybody's seen the movie. We all know what happened.

But just as a recap, for years, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] had been lowering rates. One of the reasons that they were doing that was to soften the blow from the dot bubble bursting. That wasn't an economic crisis. It was just the stock market falling.

And that blew up a new bubble in the housing market. Everybody was taking advantage of adjustable rate [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]], buying multiple houses. There was euphoria. And the banks were packaging up all of these loans into collateralized debt obligations, [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities, and selling them off to investors.

Now, most people aren't aware that the reason this blew up and became a threat to banks is because of something called the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|repo market]]. The way that banks work is that if they run out of cash at the end of the day, they will just borrow cash from another bank. And in order to borrow that cash, they will pledge an asset that they promise to repurchase in the future. That's the word repo comes from repurchase.

If this sounds like a pawn shop, it is. It's just the biggest, most complex financial pawn shop. So, a bank will give collateral to another bank in exchange for cash and say, "Hey, tomorrow or next day or next week or sometime in the future, I'll buy that collateral back from you." Now, in the financial system at that time, most of the collateral was these [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities.

But once everybody realized that the [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] weren't getting paid and these things were all going to collapse, that collateral went from being good collateral to bad collateral basically overnight. And suddenly no bank was willing to accept [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities as collateral. Which meant one morning you wake up and you're a bank and you realize I don't have the cash I need and nobody will take my collateral. I'm done for.

You instantly go under. That's why the banks started failing. And so very quickly the government orchestrated a bailout of the banks and they did so at the expense of the taxpayer.
