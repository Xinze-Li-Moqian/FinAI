# What is the Optimal Money Supply?

Have you ever heard anybody say that the solution to poverty is money? At first glance, it sounds reasonable. Hey, I'm poor. That means more money would fix my situation. But is that true? Especially across an entire system, an economy, the world. Is it possible that we could simply just print our way to universal abundance?

First of all, I hope your knowledge of history of places like Zimbabwe, Venezuela, the Vhimar Republic in Germany, heck, even the US continental would mean that, you know, we can't just print money and expect prosperity to follow. But it's also true that the opposite won't work. In other words, you can't just start destroying money and expect wealth creation to follow. So the question is, what is the optimal money supply?

And even more importantly, how should that supply change over time in order to ensure the fairest and most opportunity for wealth creation? Well, today it is the job of [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] to answer that question for everyone. And the way they answer that question is by continuously increasing the money supply at their discretion for the last h 100red years, continually making it push to all-time highs like it just recently has. But what if I were to tell you that there is no optimal money supply?

Even more so, there is no optimal rate of change in the money supply. In fact, any supply of money will work equally well. Any supply of money will work equally well so long as two things are true. Number one, the unit of money is sufficiently divisible.

And number two, the right people are in charge of determining the supply. Now, if you know me at all, that probably sounds insane that I'm saying that. So let me explain. To start answering this question, we need to imagine a few differences to the way that the dollar works today.

So let's pretend just for the sake of understanding that the dollar supply could not change for now. Right now the [[Concepts/Finance/International Finance/US Dollar in Global Finance|US dollar]] supply is $22 trillion. So let's just say it is stuck there. Money cannot be loaned into existence. When debt gets paid off, it doesn't destroy money.

The Fed or banks cannot print money. In fact, just to make this crystal clear, every dollar is actually a physical printed piece of paper dollar. The total supply is absolutely fixed. It cannot change. If one piece of paper dollar does get destroyed by something like a fire, it's instantly replaced by the Fed.

Money supply is fixed. It cannot change. When you buy or sell and you transact and money moves from a credit card to debit card, from one bank to another, there's actual physical pallets of paper money that are moving from vault to vault, from bank to bank. Okay, got the picture?

The next change we have to imagine here is that [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are set by the market only. There is nobody in charge of setting [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. So, if I want to lend somebody money, I can try charging whatever [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] I want. The only way I'm going to be able to lend money is if somebody decides they choose they want to borrow that money from me at the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] I'm offering.

Similarly, I can go try and borrow money for something for anything. And I can try and get as low of an [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] as I want. But the only way I'm going to be able to do that is if somebody with money decides that they would like to voluntarily lend me their money at the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that I want. Oh, let's say for whatever reason people are fearful about the future of the economy and so they start to save up their money, which means they're not spending.

This means there's a lot less money going around in circulation. That means a lot of people's incomes start to drop. That means demand for purchasing goes down. And that means if I want to be able to sell something so that I can get more money, I'm going to have to offer that thing at a lower price if I want a chance of being able to sell it.

In other words, when the savings pool starts to increase, there's less money in circulation. That means the active or the real the practical supply of money actually goes down and prices adjust lower to compensate. Prices of everything, prices of goods, prices of assets, even prices of [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Wages|wages]]. So now we've seen prices fall, the active supply of money decreases.

So we have in practice [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]] happening. But the money supply, the pool of savings is actually pretty large because everybody's saving. And so I'm going to go out there trying to lend my money, trying to put it to work. I'm going to realize nobody wants to borrow my money from me cuz they all have savings.

Which means if I want to lend my money, I'm going to have to charge a lower [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] in order to get anybody to take my money. And so once the savings pool gets too large, [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] will come down, which will incentivize me to actually go spend my money. It's not doing anything for me in savings. It's not making me a [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] or at least a decent enough [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]].

So, it incentivizes me to actually go spend that money and everybody else with savings. That drives that excess savings back into the economy, lifting prices back up. Now, let's push this too far. Let's say everybody keeps on doing this. rates are low, so everybody borrows and everybody spends.

Well, guess what? It will get to a point where all of that money flooding into circulation drives prices up to the point where ah, I can't borrow now anymore at the low rates that I want to because nobody else has money. I try and borrow from you. I try to borrow from him.

I try and borrow from everybody. Nobody has the excess savings anymore. So, if I want to be able to borrow money, I'm going to have to pay a really high [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]]. The savings pool just got drained, which means [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] have to adjust higher.

And that incentivizes people to save again. So yes, prices did go up when those savings flooded back into the economy. Now they'll adjust back down. You see, with a fixed supply of money and a free market and [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], there's a natural equilibrium between the value, the demand, and the supply of money.

And [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] respond automatically to market forces, making sure that the savings pool is sufficient. The incentives change as the the demand and the supply changes which keeps a good eb and flow. What I've described is how money worked for basically all of human history when money was gold. I'm not talking about paper backed by gold.

I'm talking about actual gold and [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Silver|silver]] and copper coins that traded as money. Over long periods of time, as the total stock of wealth grows, as technology increases, as efficiency and production and progress happens, it makes it easier to get your hands on more wealth. It costs you less labor to afford the same amount of food, cost you less time to afford the same amount of shelter. With a fixed money supply, over time, the prices of everything gradually drift downwards because this total stock of wealth grows faster than the total stock of money.

The difference between our hypothetical example of a fixed dollar and gold is that gold can actually have its supply increased through mining. Now, a thousand years ago, if you were going to set up as a gold miner, you might initially think, I'm going to generate free money. I'm just going to go get gold out of the ground, and that's pure [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] for me. But then you start to do the math, and you start to realize, okay, well, I have to hire some people.

We have to do the labor of digging the holes in the ground. I have to buy the equipment, the shovels and the pickaxes. And then I have to invest in a furnace and the infrastructure to be able to refine it and then melt it down, stamp it into coins or bars. And you start to do the math and you realize, man, it's going to cost me about 3 ounces of gold to produce 1 ounce of gold out of the ground.

In other words, the demand for money at that time is not very high. It is easier to put your labor and your resources to work to get existing money from someone else rather than to put those resources at work to create new money. But that's not always the case. Sometimes, like in our example before, the demand for money can go up.

In those situations, it may only cost a half an ounce of gold to produce every ounce of gold that you can out of the ground. So, in those situations, all the miners start producing gold again. This means that throughout history, the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] rate for gold was only between about 1 and 2%. This was dictated by the market market forces when demand for money was great enough that it was [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profitable]] to produce more money.

And that production stopped once it was no longer profitable to do so. Now, one of the issues with gold is that it was so valuable that people decided to start putting it in storage instead of using it for everyday transactions. The invention of paper redeemable for gold meant that you could now trade the ownership of gold in a transaction rather than transacting with the gold itself. The invention of paper redeemable for gold also had the advantage of making gold essentially infinitely divisible because on a piece of paper I could say this piece of paper is redeemable for 1 1,000th of an ounce of gold.

But if I wanted to go actually do a transaction with 1/ 1,000th of an ounce, be very difficult to actually produce that and measure it and verify it. Redeeming it would not be hard either because you would just need 1,000 of those notes to just go redeem for 1 ounce. This means that you could have infinite [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]] over time and still use [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Gold as a Monetary Asset|gold as money]] because it doesn't matter how cheap something gets. You can always write a smaller division of the gold as the amount that you are transacting with to accommodate for any cheap item no matter how cheap it gets.

Now throughout history we've had different forms of money. Gold was the dominant one for sure, but we've also seen cattle used as money, large stones used as money, seashells, rice, and of course, digital money not backed up by anything. No, I'm not talking about [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]]. I'm talking about the dollar.

So, where does money's value actually come from? Why does something like the dollar have value throughout history? Why did gold rise to have value over the last few years? Why has [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] risen in value? And why do some people expect that it will be the dominant form of money in the future?

Well, money is simply a type of good. And any good, whether it's a consumption good, an investment good, a capital good, will have a mix of value of use value and exchange value. In other words, my iPhone has value because I can use it to do stuff. And the amount I'm willing to pay to be able to do the stuff that I want to do with my iPhone would be considered its use value.

However, there is some exchange value in an iPhone if I wanted to. I could go barter it with somebody. Somebody has a nice Android phone, which I don't know if nice Android phones exist, but hypothetically if they did, I could go trade somebody and say, "Hey, would you like to do a swap?" And we could change, which means that my iPhone has some exchange value.

Throughout history, the different forms of money like rice and seashells were used as money. But part of the reason why they didn't survive is mostly because of their use value. Because rice is a food, it can deteriorate. It can rot. Because cattle is a food and it can die.

And it's very large. It's hard to trade 1/100th of a cow if it's still alive. So those forms of money did not survive as money primarily because they had too much use value. When you consider gold historically, it had zero use value. It arose primarily because of its exchange value.

In other words, due to the fact that it didn't rust. It doesn't decay. It's very easily identifiable. It's very hard to forge or to counterfeit. And its supply is only going to increase if the total market demand for money is higher than the cost of producing money.

It meant that people were willing to accept it as payment. they could hold on to it, not worry about it getting rotting or destroyed and then they could use it for payment again because that other person knew that somebody else down the road would also accept it as payment. And so in some sense for most of human history, it was the lack of use value and the entire value of gold came from its exchange value that secured its place as money. Now I know a lot of people will say, well, it was used ornamental and jewelry and coding stuff.

A lot of that actually has to do with it being money, with it being scarce and valuable. So you could make an argument that its use value in things like jewelry historically were a result of its exchange value, not the other way around. Now today, different story. There is a lot of use value in gold and things like electronics, but historically that's what caused it to become secured in that place as the money for thousands of years.

As a side note, many criticize [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] for having no use value, but that's exactly what characterized gold. The value comes from its moneyiness of it being a store of value of it being a medium of exchange of it being the having the ability to be used for payments. It comes from its moneyiness, not from its use for other things. And in fact, this is exactly the position the dollar is in today.

The dollar's value is only exchange value. There is no use value in the dollar. What are you going to use it for? Toilet paper? Next time there's a lockdown, you're going to start a fire with it like your Pablo Escobar? And most dollars aren't even paper.

They're digital, only exchange value. And yet, it's the [[Concepts/Finance/International Finance/Reserve Currencies|global reserve currency]], and it's been that way for decades, and it doesn't look like it's slowing down anytime soon. So, money has very little or no use value. It only has exchange value. So, we can say for certain that if you increase the money supply, you do not increase the use value in the economy, in the society, in the world.

For example, if you increase the housing supply, you do increase the use value in the world. There's more houses that people can use. If you increase the car supply, you increase the use value in total in the world. There's more cars people can have.

If you increase the food supply, you increase the use value in total in the world. There's more food that people can eat. If you increase the money supply, no more use value because money doesn't have use value. Okay, but what about its exchange value?

If you increase the money supply in the world, does it actually increase in total the net exchange value that exists in the world? Well, exchange value only means it has value because it's waiting to be exchanged for something else of real value. You use money to exchange for something you want like somebody's time, somebody's labor, somebody's product, somebody's food, some the clothes somebody made, the house somebody built, the car somebody made. So the exchange value of money is a relative value compared to all of the actual stuff that exists in the world.

Which means if you increase the supply of money, you only dilute the exchange value of the existing money, it does not add either use value to the world. Therefore, it does not add exchange value to the world. Which means that when you increase the supply of money, whether gold, [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] or dollars, it neither increases society's total use value nor the total exchange value. It has always been this way.

When gold got mined for thousands of years when it was being used as money, it diluted the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] of the existing ounces of gold. That's why mining would stop because eventually you've brought enough new coins into circulation that it has stopped the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]] enough that the demand for money stops being so high because the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] of that money has been diluted. Now, this wasn't a bad thing. It kept the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] of gold where the market wanted it.

So then what would happen if the demand for money increases but the supply doesn't? Let's say our example from earlier where the the supply of dollars is capped or even in a [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] standard world. Well then prices of everything else will have to drop because money's value is just exchange value. And so if the demand for money is growing that means the other way you can say that is that the prices of everything else are dropping.

In other words, you just get the opposite of what we've had for the last hundred years. Instead of prices slowly increasing and sometimes fastly quickly increasing over time, you have prices that slowly decrease over time. And this is why you need a money that is sufficiently divisible because as things get cheaper, you need to be able to pay for them with smaller and smaller parts of the money. The world we live in today with [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Fiat Money|fiat money]], dollars that come into or out of existence by declaration from a central committee of people who think they know what's best for the rest of the world more than what the rest of the world knows for themselves.

The value, the cost of money, the supply of money are all chosen for you. They decide in their ivory tower what you should pay to be able to get your hands on more money. what you should pay for all goods by choosing how rapidly to expand the supply of your money and therefore dilute your [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]]. Now, you might think that if they're good enough, they'll just choose the correct supply and value of money.

But guess what? Not only will they never do that, it's actually impossible. That's right. The ability for the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] to choose a supply and cost of money that the market would choose is not possible. But guess what? It's irrelevant anyway. Because the very purpose of a [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]], the reason they exist is to ensure that the supply and the value of money is different than what the market would do.

Otherwise, they would just let the market do it. Their reason for being is to make sure that it is different. Which means they're constantly choosing the winners and the losers on purpose. And if they were choosing the supply and the value of money that the market would choose, it would simply be like a guy standing in a grocery store as somebody's checking out shouting the prices of the items as they're ringing up on the cash register pretending like he's controlling them.

He's not. That's the market. The purpose of the Fed and other [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] is to actually go in and change the prices themselves. And that's what they do. This means by definition you have malinvestment, misallocation of resources, people making choices with their money that they wouldn't if they were getting the proper signals.

[[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|Interest rates]] are either too high or too low. You are incentivized to either spend money, borrow money, save money, or lend money in a way that is actually different than reality. Your incentives are wrong. People are constantly perceiving money to either be less valuable or more valuable than it actually is.

Which means everybody is constantly making incorrect choices as a result of this intervention. Which means that by definition, any intervention on the supply and value of money necessarily causes an economy to produce less wealth than it could otherwise. Or set another way, the most amount of wealth that can possibly be produced across the entire world would be under a system where both the supply and the cost, the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] of money are determined solely by the free market, by all individual actors with no restriction or choice forced on them by anyone else.

Which means the solution to poverty is not money. The solution to poverty is wealth, is goods, is the most production of what people want and need. Which means the solution to alleviate the most possible poverty is a completely free market in money. As always, thank you so much for watching.
