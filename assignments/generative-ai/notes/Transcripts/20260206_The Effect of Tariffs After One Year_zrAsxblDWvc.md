# The Effect of Tariffs After One Year

There seems to be a lot of confusion out there about exactly how much money [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] have been bringing in to the United States so far. And it's not your fault because the numbers that are being thrown around are entirely and widely inaccurate. So, let's take a look at the real numbers, the actual data about how much money [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] have been bringing in. First chart I want you to look at is a year-to-date chart.

This chart shows how much [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue the United States has brought in so far in this calendar year, and it also compares that to prior years at the exact same time. You can see at the bottom of this chart we have 2022, 23, 24, 25, and they're all right in line with each other. There is virtually no significant difference in the amount of [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue brought in for all those years. However, you can see at the very top of the chart we have 2026.

And as of January 23rd, 2026, the US has brought in over $7 billion in [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue. It's well over triple what was brought in in any year prior. So, it is a fact that the United States is bringing in much more [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue, both as a percent and as a raw dollar amount, than any other year. Now, if we look back at last year, the entire year in 2025, we can also see it was a monster year in terms of [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue. $288 billion was the total collected [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue.

You can see that dwarfs every prior year in comparison, with the largest prior year being 2022 with a 115 billion dollars in [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue. Compared to prior years, on average, 2025 brought in about three times as much money in [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]], which is a big jump. So, how does this big jump in [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue compare to everything else that the US government takes in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]]? Cuz remember, [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] are a [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]].

The definition of a [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] is money the government charges from you to do something, whether that is to own property, to buy or sell goods, to earn income, to sell an asset, or to buy something from another country, it's a [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]]. If we take a look at how much money the US government has taken in [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] compared to all [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue, we can see that prior years it was very steadily in between 2% and 3%. However, starting in April of 2025, the percentage of [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue started to climb. And [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] at the end of 2025 ended up being a whopping 3.72% of all collected [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue.

But over here at the top left of the chart, you can see a line that looks like an error on this chart, just hovering there in the middle of nowhere. And that's because [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] were in full effect already, starting in fiscal year 2026. Which means that as of December of 2025, which is 3 months into the fiscal year of 2026, [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] had made up a whopping 7.35% of total collected [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue. Now, this number will certainly go down after April of 2026, because that's when all of the regular [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue comes in, or the largest chunk of it.

However, 2026 will almost certainly represent a year in which [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue makes up a record percentage of all [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue the federal government collects, beating out the last record of 2025 at 3.72%. Okay, but now for the hard news. 4, 5, 6, 7% is almost meaningless. If the number of dollars that you paid in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]] went down by 7% as a result of this, it would make very little difference in your life.

And that is a false if, because your [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] payments are not going down as a result of [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]]. The government isn't changing its spending, its borrowing, and its taxing as a result of this. So, this isn't lowering your [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] bill. This is just extra money the government has started taxing.

And it's still only 7%. Even if you have a relatively large income compared to the rest of the US population, and you earned a couple hundred thousand dollars in 2025, your total federal [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] bill, let's say, was a hundred thousand dollars. A reduction of 7% means you would have saved seven thousand dollars. Now, seven thousand dollars is not nothing, but for somebody who's already paying a hundred thousand dollar [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] bill, that extra seven grand is not going to meaningfully change their life.

Similarly, if you have a much lower income, and you paid a total of ten thousand dollars in federal [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]], and you were able to get a reduction of seven hundred dollars, again, not going to change your life. So, while [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue does make up 7% of current [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] revenue so far for fiscal year 2026, which is a record compared to past years, it's not even a drop in the bucket. It doesn't move the needle. It doesn't change anything about your life.

And even if it did result in a dollar-for-dollar reduction in the amount of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]] you were going to pay, which it won't, but even if it did, it still wouldn't make a difference in your life. But most importantly, it's not making a difference in what the US government is doing. Not only is there no reduction in the amount of money that the government is taking from you in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]], and not only is there no reduction in the total amount of money the US government is spending, but this extra [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue is still not even making a dent in the actual deficit, which is the amount that the government has to borrow because they're spending so much after they take everything from you in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]].

Year-to-date, they've already racked up a six hundred billion dollar deficit, which is right in line with the deficit during prior years at this time. So, comparing the current [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue to [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue from past years is irrelevant. So, the questions that people should be asking, the questions that actually matter are, is the government taking less money from you in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]] because of the [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]]? No. Is the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Government Spending|government spending]] less money because of the [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]]?

No. Is the government borrowing less money because of the [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]]? No. The [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariff]] revenue is not making any meaningful change to how much the government takes from you and how much the government spends. And guess what? It's not even possible for that to happen.

Because if [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] were raised to the point that it could meaningfully reduce the deficit, reduce the spending, reduce how much the government takes from you in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]], what would end up happening far before that point is that imports would basically go to zero. Because [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Fiscal Policy|fiscal policy]] changes behavior. [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|Taxes]] disincentivize behavior. The reason why the US government has [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]] on things like alcohol and cigarettes is to discourage people from buying those things, to make it harder, make it more expensive.

The reason why people who have the ability to choose don't take their wealth in terms of earned income is because earned income is discouraged by punishing it through [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]]. And so if buying stuff from overseas was so punished so highly that it became so expensive that it could make a dent in the budget, people would not buy stuff from overseas anymore. And they would buy it domestically. And I know there's a lot of you who think that would be a great thing.

But the reason why people buy stuff from other people in other countries right now is because it's cheaper. The definition of wealth is being able to afford the stuff we want and need. And so if you can buy something for $100, that is better for you than buying the same thing for $1,000. When prices are lower, that means you can afford more of the stuff you want and need.

And so by the government coming in and artificially imposing extra prices to say no, you can't get that cheap thing anymore for how much it really costs, we're going to make it so much more expensive that you have to get it from somebody else who can't make it that cheaply, you have to pay more for it, that makes your life worse, not better. And sure, it might make the lives of domestic producers of certain things better because they are now artificial winners that the government has chosen by decree. But that is not better for everybody.

That is cronyism. It's the same thing that results in military contractors being artificially wealthier because the government chose them. Or Big Pharma being artificially wealthier because the government chose them. It's cronyism. It is corruption. It is not free markets and it does not result in greater wealth, greater equality, or better growth.

It harms those things. We need less government, less intervention, and fewer [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]] everywhere. And that's not going to be accomplished by trying to make this number go higher on the chart. So, the net result of [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Tariffs|tariffs]] 1 year in are that life is slightly worse for everybody overall.

And let's just hope it stops there. As always, thank you so much for watching. Have a great day.
