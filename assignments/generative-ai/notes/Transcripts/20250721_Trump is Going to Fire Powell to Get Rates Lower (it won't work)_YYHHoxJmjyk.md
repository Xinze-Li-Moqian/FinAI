Now, it's no secret that ever since the inauguration, Trump has been pressuring the Fed and specifically Powell to lower rates. Uh recently a scandal about how much the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has been spending on renovations at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] building could give Trump the ammunition he needs in order to fire Powell.

If you don't remember, months ago, Trump actually looked into whether or not he could fire Powell. And due to policy reasons, it was pretty much a straightforward answer. No, you don't have the authority to remove him. However, with something like this used as the scapegoat to remove Powell, it might actually work.

Apparently, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has spent $2.5 billion on a renovation plan. And Trump told a reporter that he thinks it sort of is a fireball offense, specifically because it seems that Powell may have lied to Congress when he said that the renovation would not include some of the upgrades that were described in the official plan documents that came out in 2021, which included things like Italian beehives and new marble finishes. Now, if you see something like that and it kind of shocks you a little bit that a government or quasi government, quasi private building would need to have such ornate finishes, then it's probably just because you aren't familiar with how the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] operates.

It is outside the economy. It doesn't rely on [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]]. It doesn't rely on [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profits]]. It doesn't need to generate revenue from sales. It is the entity that creates the money. It spends what it wants and needs to spend because it doesn't need to give anything of value whatsoever in order to get that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]].

Now, you might think, hey, well, that's just the government overall. But even the government at the end of the day is full of people who are at least appointed by elected officials who have to win votes. That does not exist at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. Good times or bad, [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Recessions|recession]] or boom.

The employees at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] sit on their nice salaries doing their little research. It is the largest employer of economic PhDs in the world. They take their breaks. They clock out. They don't get their perks cut when the economy is down or the business is struggling.

That does not exist for the Fed. So seeing plans for upgrades like Italian beehives and new marble finishes should not be surprising. Okay, but getting back to Trump firing Powell specifically to try and get rates lower. It's not clear that this will actually accomplish the goal that Trump thinks it will because as of right now, the yield on the 30-year US Treasury has breached 5% yet again.

The 20-year has also breached 5% and the 10-year is looking dangerously close to a breakout that could also get it back up to 5% which it briefly touched in October of 2023. Now, because these long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are so high, the US government has not been issuing a ton of debt at this maturity. There's simply not enough liquidity in the markets, not enough lenders and money willing to lend to the government for that length of time at those high [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. And so instead, for years now, the government has been rolling over all of its debt that's been maturing into short-term debt, Ta bills, debt that matures in under two years, usually under one year.

Now, it's not like this debt has that much of a better [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]]. A six-month T bill is yielding 4.2% right now, but the idea is that it's constantly rolling over, which means if those long-term rates get to go down at some point soon, then when all of that short-term debt then finally matures within the next couple of months or the year, then they'll have all that debt that they can refinance into longerterm debt once those [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are lower. They don't want to lock up long-term rates for 10, 20, 30 years right now when they're still so high.

And Trump has committed to rolling over basically all the debt that matures into short-term T bills until [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] go down. One of the reasons why he's been pressuring Powell to lower rates. But taking a look at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]], we can see what happened last time the Fed lowered rates leading up to the election in 2024. The Fed lowered the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Fed funds rate]], which is the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] it controls, from an effective rate of 5.3% down to 4.3%.

That happened from August of 2024 through January of 2025. However, if we look at what that did to long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], it took those long-term yields from 4% all the way up to 5% over that exact same time frame. So, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] lowering rates, the one rate that they control, which is the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Fed funds rate]], actually had the impact of increasing [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for that longer term debt. Which is why you get long-term rates actually go up. because those long-term rates as of right now are still at least partially controlled by free market influences, people buying and selling those [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]].
