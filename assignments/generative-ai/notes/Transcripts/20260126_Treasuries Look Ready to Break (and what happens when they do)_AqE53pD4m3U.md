# Treasuries Look Ready to Break (and what happens when they do)

Treasury market looks ready to break, and when it does, here's what you should expect will happen. Right now, as of the time of this recording, gold is on the brink of breaking $5,000 per ounce. Given the fact that gold has acted as a monetary asset for thousands of years, and is still today by [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] and large funds around the world. This is a sign that more and more are putting their trust and faith in the safe haven asset, the safe haven asset, rather than something like the dollar or [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], which have acted temporarily as a safe haven asset for the last few decades.

Compared to world history, this is a blip on the radar. But, it's not just gold signaling a problem here. We're also seeing traditional assets like [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] continue to push to all-time highs. You know, many people are worried about bubbles in real estate, bubbles in crypto, bubbles in [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]], bubbles all over the place.

But, the thing is, if everything is a bubble, nothing is. Maybe it's not that the assets are in a bubble, maybe it's a problem with the denominator. Maybe your measuring stick is shrinking. Maybe the dollar is exhibiting signs of weakness. Now, it's true that the dollar index over the last couple of years has been moving somewhat lower.

But, the dollar index is simply a measure of the dollar versus other currencies. If you want a sign of true weakness or strength in the dollar, you have to look at the other things that you can compare it against, namely the things that you purchase with it. And whether we're looking at pretty much any asset class, or whether we're looking at the cost of living through things like housing, transportation, or food, the dollar is getting much, much weaker. And that is a problem because it has been making borrowing in those dollars for the government more and more expensive.

You see, the reason why the dollar is getting weaker compared to all the stuff that we care about, like assets and goods, is because so many more of them are continuing to come into existence. Money supply keeps on it making new all-time highs. As new money comes into existence, it bids up the prices of everything because you have more money chasing the same amount of goods and services. Now, obviously, we still have growing goods and services.

Production increases over time. But, if the money supply grows faster than the supply of all the stuff, then the stuff will get more expensive. This also means that if you want to borrow those dollars, it will be more expensive to do so. You see, if I'm going to lend you $100, and in a year you're going to pay me back a 1% [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]], I'm going to get $101 back from you.

That sounds fine. I'm going to have a 1% [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]]. But, over that same time period, let's say the cost of living increases by 5%, which means I used to be able to go out to dinner for $100. Now, it's going to cost me $105 to get the exact same dinner.

Well, I'm no longer going to be willing to lend you money at 1% anymore. I'm going to demand at least 5% cuz otherwise, in real terms, I'm losing. That is exactly why borrowing for the US government has been getting more and more expensive because lenders are not willing to part with their dollars for 10, 20, 30 years unless they believe they are being compensated for that loss of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]]. Now, you might take a look at something like short-term [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and notice that it's gotten a lot cheaper for the government to borrow.

And that is specifically because of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] controls the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Fed funds rate]], which is the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that banks earn or get charged to lend money to each other overnight. And when the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] pushes that [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] lower, it helps suppress other [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] that are close to that along the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]]. In other words, if the overnight rate goes down to 1%, then that will cause the six-month rate or the one-year rate to also go down.

Not as much, but somewhat. However, if the overnight rate goes up to 10%, well, the other rates will also go up. Again, maybe not as much, but it has an influence, which is why the government can borrow for six months at a much lower rate now because the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] had been lowering [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. But again, it's been having the opposite effect on the longer end of the curve, as we can see with the 10-year yield.

And as an inverse to yields, we can see the price of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] has been going down, which has caused [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] funds like TLT to also go down. And if you look more closely at the 10-year yield, you can see recently it's been getting higher quickly, and it looks like it's about to break out. Now, there are a couple of reasons for this. Number one is future [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]].

Because right now, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] may be temporarily suppressed. But, given the rate of government borrowing and spending and the money supply increase, and the expectations on how much that might impact growth, lenders are demanding more and more to lock their money up for longer and longer. And we can see this clearly when we take a look at the composition of the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]]. The government has been forced to borrow more and more with short-term debt, and they're just not able to find enough borrowers at the long end.

To take a look at the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] broken down by type, we can see that only 17% of the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] is currently held in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|Bonds]] are the 30-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and the 20-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. And we can see that 50% of the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] is in notes. Notes are 2-year, 3-year, 5-year, 7-year, and 10-year.

And now, 21% of the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] is in bills. Bills are short-term from 4-week maturity up to 52-week maturity. So, almost 72% of the entire [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] is held at the short end of the curve, maturing in under 10 years. And increasingly frequently, more and more of that is at 2 years or less.

Obviously, we hear stories all the time of different organizations, funds, or countries moving away from investing in US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. Just recently, it was announced that a Danish [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Pensions|pension]] fund would be selling off its entire treasury holding. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said that Denmark's investment in the US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury bonds]], like Denmark itself, is irrelevant. And amount-wise, he's right.

It is true. It is irrelevant to the size of the US Treasury market. But, it's also true that this is a signpost. It's a little signal, and we keep on seeing more and more of these. The biggest one I mentioned earlier is simply gold accumulation.

And as you can see, just recently, official reserves of US debt have now been overtaken by reserves held in gold. This is not something that happens overnight, but it is something that is happening. So, what will happen when [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] break out to the upside, and the US government cannot find enough lenders to borrow at the rates that it needs to afford? It will go to the place it always does, the printer.

Although it may look differently this time. [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] historically has done [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] whenever the US government needs it to buy more of its debt. And just recently, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] started increasing its balance sheet yet again, as you can see at the end of this chart. They are buying [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] once again because nobody else is stepping up to the plate.

But, given what happened the last time the Fed did a huge round of [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]], this would be very unpopular for them to come right out and start buying up a bunch of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] again, which means the next time this happens and accelerates, it's probably going to come from the banks. Governor Moran at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has talked about this extensively in speeches and reports that you can find on the Fed's website, basically saying that we need to free up banks' balance sheets so that they can buy more US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]].

Right now, banks are restricted in the number of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] they can buy because of something called the supplementary leverage ratio. That was temporarily suspended back in 2020 and allowed banks to buy as many [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] as they wanted, and they did. But, that suspension was only temporary, and the amount of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] that they can own now is restricted again. And the argument is they need to be deregulated permanently so that they can go out there and buy as many [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] as they want.

And the thing is, they will because they're using your money to do it. So, they don't care that they're only getting 4.2% or if they're able to drive rates lower, they won't care if they're only getting 3 and 1/2% or 3%. All they care about is the difference between what they get from the government and what they have to pay you because they're not using their money, they're using yours. So, if they're getting 3 and 1/2% from government [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], they'll pay you 0.3% or 0.1% or maybe nothing.

And the only thing holding them back from doing this right now is the law. And that law will likely change as soon as the government needs it to. So, treasury yields look ready to break out to the upside, which means temporarily looks like [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] are ready to break out to the downside. And when they do, it's likely that banks will be unleashed to buy them all up and earn an unlimited, an infinite return using your money.

And when this happens, brace for impact because the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] this might unleash could be severe. As always, thank you so much for watching. Have a great day.
