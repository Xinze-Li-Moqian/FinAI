# Are We About to See Stagflation in the US?

Many people are justifiably scared about stagflation with the oil shock reminding everybody about what happened in the 70s, the reminders of what happens when [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] starts running out of control and what the Fed may do in response to that with hiking [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. People are understandably afraid of a decade of rising prices and a stagnating economy. So first of all, what is stagflation, how did it show up last time, and then we're going to talk about are we seeing things more similar to the 70s right now or more different and what we can expect policy and the markets and the economy to do as a result.

First of all, take a look at this chart. This is stagflation from 1965 through 1985. Really, this is a chart of the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]] only right now. So we see 1965 [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] was reading at 1.2% rose before it peaked in 1970 at 6.3% then it briefly fell to bottom at 3% in 72.

Rose again to a peak of 9.1% in 1975, fell again to a bottom of 6.3% in 77, and then peaked for the last time at 12.4% in 1980 before Volcker finally crushed it with high [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and it started heading down from there. This may be a more readable chart for you and it's longer term as well so you can see [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] and [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Headline and Core Inflation|core inflation]] rose and then fell and then rose and then fell and then rose again before peaking in 1980 and then we get to closer where we are today.

Now let's take a look at what GDP did during that time. So you can see GDP was well above 5% and then dipped to a bottom of 0.2% in 1970. Rose again to get back over 5% by 1973 and we had a couple quarters or years of declining GDP in 74 and 75. Rose back up above 5% and then it dipped below to negative growth again in 1980 and in 1982 before it resumed its growth.

So pretty volatile GDP growth and this is maybe a more readable chart for you with the real GDP percentage change from the year before and the frequency here on this chart is quarterly. The fact that it is real means that it is adjusted for [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Consumer Price Index (CPI)|CPI]]. Now, seeing things from a longer-term perspective like this allows you to see more of the trend. Like I always say, when in doubt, zoom out.

And without knowing that you are looking for something called stagflation happening in the '70s, you might not really notice anything out of the ordinary. GDP growth was pretty volatile through this entire time, spiking up to topping out really around 7 to 8% quite regularly, and then bottoming out with some negative GDP growth quite regularly. There's really nothing happening in the '70s that stands out as abnormal from what was happening before. Further, even after that, really nothing abnormal happens.

Although, you do see a trend of less volatility and also lower and lower highs. We've seen GDP in a very tight range for the last two decades. Obviously, if we exclude the bottom in 2020 and the peak in 2021, we've been in a very tight range compared to what GDP used to do. And here's a chart of [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]] during that exact same time frame.

We can see [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]] was dropping until about 1969, and then it started rising before it peaked in 1982. So, stagflation is something that prior to the '70s, most economists thought couldn't happen. This was a rise in [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]] while having a rise in [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. Prior to the '70s, economists just assumed those things were impossible to happen together.

Either [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] rose while [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Unemployment|unemployment]] dropped or vice versa. Ben Bernanke said that the '70s really caused the Fed's credibility as an [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] fighter to be lost. He said the unmooring of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]] greatly complicated the process of making [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]]. In particular, Fed's loss of credibility significantly increased the cost of achieving disinflation.

Disinflation is the rate of price increases moving lower, even though prices are still going up. The actual [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] number goes down. If we take a look at this chart, which is the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]], that is the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] controls, we can see that they hiked it, dropped it, hiked it, dropped it, and then hiked it finally to a peak of almost 20% in 1981, and that is what finally cracked [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] and started bringing it lower. Now, the reason why people are afraid of stagflation right now is obviously because we are still feeling the effects of the rapid growth in the money supply from 2020, 2021, and on, but a lot of it also has to do with the oil shock.

Obviously, there's a lot going on in the Middle East right now impacting the prices of oil, and that is exactly the same type of thing that was happening in the '70s. We had the Arab oil embargo, crude oil prices spiked in 1973. We also had things like the Iranian Revolution, and so there were a lot of things going on with oil in that part of the world that remind people about what happened back then. However, there is a very, very big difference between the world right now, specifically the government's financial position, that doesn't allow the same sort of policies that they did back in the '70s in order to crack the back of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]].

What I'm talking about, you probably already know where I'm going, and that is the debt-to-GDP ratio for the United States government. The debt-to-GDP ratio following World War II was up above 120%, and because of everybody coming home from the war, the wartime spending stopping, [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control, capital controls, [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Financial Repression|financial repression]], and inflating away the debt, they were able to take the next 40 30 to 40 years and bring that debt-to-GDP ratio back down below 40%, and it bottomed around 30%. The debt-to-GDP ratio is extremely important to consider here because it is the United States government's debt load compared to the size of the US economy.

That is what the US government is able to [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]]. And so, when the debt load gets super high relative to the size of the economy, it means the government cannot deal with that debt load through [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxation]] alone. It must print over the debt. Must inflate its debt load away if it doesn't want to default.

That was not the case in the '70s. So, when [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] started roaring out of control, the way that they dealt with that was hiking [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] to the point where prices started to get crushed. Now, this is also flawed thinking because it ignores the production side, which I'll talk about in just a moment. But, Volker was able to raise [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] to the point where it finally stopped the rise of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. And the US government could afford that because even though [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] were going up so much, they really didn't have that much debt relative to the size of the economy.

In fact, it was below pre-World War II levels. So, even though [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] were going up, the government could afford to continue to pay the bills, even though it had to continue to roll over its debt at higher and higher costs. It'd be like if you made a hundred grand a year and you had $30,000 worth of debt. Yes, an increase in the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] you were paying on that would suck, but it wouldn't completely bankrupt you.

However, if you make a hundred grand a year and you owe $120,000 in debt, even a modest increase in the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] might bankrupt you cuz you can only make your minimum payments. In fact, the US government right now can't even make their minimum payments. They have to borrow more from a new credit card just to make their minimum payments and also pay their other bills. That's the situation you get in when your debt is above 120%, which means right now with the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Fed funds rate]] sitting under 4%, you do not have the ability for the Fed to do the same thing that they did in the '70s if [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] starts running out of control.

Now, here's a couple of things that some people are missing with this. [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|Inflation]] is largely driven by an increase in the money supply that outpaces the [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Production|production of goods]] and services. That's important. Again, I'll talk about that in a moment, but it's mainly driven by an increase in the money supply.

That's why when they increased the money supply by a record 25% in 2020 and 2021, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] started running out of control. Also, if you look at what the money supply did during the Great Financial Crisis, it really did not increase that much. That's one of the reasons why we didn't see out of control [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] back then. Even though they did a lot of money printing, it didn't result in the overall money supply increasing.

Basically, they printed base money, like [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]], and that allowed the money supply to not collapse, but they didn't directly increase it. Very different than what they did in 2020 and 2021, where they just printed money, paid out checks, PPP loans, things like that to get cash flowing in the economy. That's direct [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] without an increase in production goods and services. More money chasing the same amount of stuff, prices go up.

That is very different than what is happening right now with something like an oil shock, where prices of some things go up without them being caused by an increase in the money supply. For example, if your rent today doubled, you might be able to continue paying the same amount of money for all the other stuff that you're buying for a little while while you burn through your savings, but eventually, if it stays like that, you will have to cut back spending in other areas. The same thing is happening right now.

If oil prices stay super high, but we don't also see an increase in the money supply, you don't get a sustained increase in all prices across the board. You get a redirection of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]]. That means pain and suffering in other areas, prices of other things come down to compensate. You can only have a broad level of price increases if you have an increase in the money supply to support that.

Now, we do see the money supply increasing again if we zoom in on just the last couple of years. We see the money supply is increasing, but there is no massive increase happening recently that is any different than what's been going on for the last couple of years, which means that overall, at least right now, it's not enough to compensate for the redirection of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] that would happen if the oil price stays where it is or higher. Now, I mentioned that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] cannot raise [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] because that would basically bankrupt the government because the debt to GDP ratio is so high.

Just the interest alone on the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] would go to $2 trillion, $3 trillion, or way more if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] tried to do anything close to what they did in the '70s with the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]]. But, the other thing that this discussion has ignored so far is the production side. When they did all the money printing in 2020 and 2021. That did not lead to any immediate increase in production because they just handed people money and they were able to go spend that on the stuff that already existed.

Then that caused shortages. It took a long time for production to make up that gap and by the time it did, the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] had already been spent through. So then you had a glut, you had the [[Finance/11 Employment and the Real Economy/Recessions|recession]], you had the drop. The boom made the bust inevitable.

But the way that the lowering of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and the easing happens matters here. Again, handing checks to people is very different than reprinting base money supply. And so in order to deal with the debt to GDP ratio being so high and getting the government lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], a lot of people are saying, and me included, I think it'll happen this year, bank deregulation is coming. [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] wants to get away from trying to manage [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] by manipulating its balance sheet and sending it higher through [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]].

But we need somebody to step in and buy all these [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. That's going to be the banks. By making a few tweaks to the way that banks are regulated, they'll be able to buy as many [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] as they want, which will push down [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for the government and that'll free up the risk limits on their balance sheet to do private lending. That lending only happens because there's a [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] incentive there.

It only happens productively, which means that any increase in the money supply won't just be directly handing cash to people to spend. It will be financing production. An increase in production can actually bring down prices, which means that if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] lowers [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] a bit and they deregulate the banks to have the banks do [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] for them, could basically see [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] drop as a result of an easier [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] environment. That could bring the debt to GDP ratio down, allowing the federal government to inflate away its debt, while at the same time increasing real GDP because GDP goes up from more production and [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] comes down.

Again, from more production, that production outpacing the increase in the money supply. Now, I'm not saying this is a definite thing, but I'm saying it is a lot more likely than many people expect right now, especially because we are all burned with that PTSD from what happened in 2020, 2021 and we have some long historical memory of what happened in the 1970s. Just know that those results are not baked into the cake. And we could see a very different, even a very positive result over the coming quarters and coming years.
