# Is MicroStrategy a Ponzi Scheme?

The definition of a Ponzi scheme is a form of fraud in which belief in the success of a non-existent enterprise is fostered by the payment of quick returns to the first investors from money invested by later investors. In other words, a fake business where there's no real business activity happening and the person running this business collects new money from new investors promising an unrealistic or unusually high return uses the money from those new investors in order to pay the return to the original investors. Micro Strategy is a [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] treasury company, one that famously raises money by issuing debt, and more recently has started issuing new preferred shares of stock that pay dividends higher than the typical stock pays in dividends ranging from 8 to 10%.

And here's the key, pays those dividends and the interest payments on its debt from the money that it raises by issuing those preferred shares and the debt. This has caused many people to make the association and say it's clear as day Michael Sailor and his company Strategy are running a Ponzi scheme. But there are a few key differences between a real fraudulent Ponzi scheme and what Strategy is doing here. First of all, the company Micro Strategy itself is a software company.

In other words, there is a real business. It makes up a very small part of their overall earnings these days, but there is a legitimate business here. Number two, they are taking the money they are raising from investors and buying [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] with the vast majority of it. A real Ponzi scheme is only using money it raises from investors, holding on to it, and using it to pay off old investors just long enough to keep the grift going.

Micro Strategy is taking as much of the money it raises as it can to purchase [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]]. And as long as [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] keeps doing what it has always done and goes up over time, then it shouldn't be a problem. And the third difference here is that this is being done with preferred shares. I'm going to explain more in detail how these work.

But the dividend payments of 8% or 10% on these preferred shares can be shut off at any time. Which means investors are not being fraudulently manipulated into investing in something that they think is a guaranteed rate of return. They know or at least they should know that the dividends on preferred can be shut off at any time if the company cannot pay it. So for these three reasons, number one, Micro Strategy has a real business even though it's small.

Number two, it's actually taking the money it raises from investors and doing something with it. In this case, buying [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]]. And number three, the returns or the income promised to investors can be cancelled at any time. It means that it does not qualify as a Ponzi scheme.

Even though the income paid out in the dividends on these preferreds is only being paid by raising more money from new investors, still doesn't count. However, that doesn't necessarily mean that this is a good idea for you to invest in these preferreds. So, I'm going to explain to you exactly how these things work so you can make up your own mind. In order to understand how preferred shares work and what the risks and benefits are, first we have to start off with debt.

If any company or let's say any individual for that matter wants to raise money in order to invest in something to grow their business, usually the easiest way to do that is with debt. You go to the bank or companies will go to public markets and they will borrow as much money as they can at the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that people are willing to provide. When public companies do this, it's called issuing [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. And then those debt instruments can be traded and can be bought and sold.

And whoever owns that debt gets paid the debt back by the company plus interest. Micro Strategy has historically issued a lot of debt. And of course, they've used basically all of it to buy as much [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] as possible. Currently, the total market cap of Micro Strategy is a little under $100 billion, which is still larger than the total value of all the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] they hold.

And the reason why the market is giving Micro Strategy a larger market cap than its total [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] that it holds is because of the expectation that the company will continue to buy more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] in the future. Markets are future discounting machines. And so investors in Micro Strategy believe that the shares even though they're more expensive than the value of the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] right now are worth it in the long run for the amount of [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] that those shares will represent in the future. And the primary way that Micro Strategy is raising money in order to buy more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] is by issuing more debt.

Now that brings us to the second thing that we need to understand before talking about preferred which is [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|common stock]]. This chart that we've been looking at for Micro Strategy is a chart of their common shares. This represents outright ownership in the company itself which means you have a full unlimited upside to all the future [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profits]] and you have full unlimited downside to all the future risks. You are literally a part owner of that company and your percentage ownership is the number of shares that you have in that company divided by the total number of shares.

Now, companies can issue what are called preferred shares, which are like a blend of debt or [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]] and [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|common stock]] shares. Micro Strategy has issued quite a few of these. They have STRD, what they call Stride, that pays a 10% dividend. STRK, which they call Strike, that pays an 8% dividend.

STRC, they call Stretch, pays a 10% dividend. And STRF, I think they call it Strife, and that pays a 10% dividend. Now you'll see on each of these there's an effective yield right now that is different than the stated dividend. Sometimes the effective yield is higher.

In the case of STRD the effective yield right now is 12.7% versus the stated dividend of 10 and STRF that has an effective yield of 9% versus the stated dividend of 10. Now the reason for this is because the share price moving up and down for preferred influences what the dividend will be for new shareholders. You can see STRF right now is trading at around $111 per share, while STRD is trading around $78 per share. So, with the same number of dollars being paid out as a dividend on each share, if you buy those shares at a lower price, your personal income that you receive on it will be a higher percentage of the money you invested.

Now, returns like this can seem wildly attractive to new investors. And so, we have to talk about what happens in a worstc case scenario. In other words, what if the company is forced to liquidate everything and return its money to its investors and its lenders? Where do preferreds sit in that mix?

Well, in the case of any company going bankrupt, shutting its doors, the first people in line to get their money back are always lenders. Lenders always have the first right to get paid back. And so, let's say something crazy happens. [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] crashes to zero.

Micro Strategy is forced to shut down and return money to everybody. The first people who will get their money back is the lenders. The [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] holders will get their money back. If there is anything left over, then the next people in line are actually the preferred shareholders.

That's why they're called preferreds. And then if there is anything left over after that, then it goes to the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|common stock]] shareholders. Now, as you can see, we have moved from least risky [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] holders to middle preferreds to most risky common shareholders because in the event of the company shutting down, those common shareholders are the last people in line to get anything back. Which means if there's not enough left over for everybody, then they're the ones who walk away with nothing.

This makes sense because as an owner, first you have to pay back who you borrowed from. And as a shareholder, a common shareholder, you are an owner. So, you're the last one to walk away with anything. It also means that the total potential return for all three of these groups is different.

When you are a [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] holder, the maximum you can get back is the principal plus the interest. There is no upside. So you have the lowest downside and also the lowest upside. If you are a common shareholder, you are a part owner. So if the company does extremely well in the future and the value of the company 10x's or 100x's, you're an owner.

You get to participate in 100% of that upside. Preferred shareholders sit in between. They have more upside potential than the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] holders do, but they don't have near as much upside potential as the common shareholders do. Part of the reason for this is because many times those preferred shares are callable.

Meaning the company at any time in the future if they want they can buy back those shares at a specific predetermined price. Which means if the company starts to do extremely well in the future and the share price of those preferred shares starts to go up too much, it might not mean you get to keep it because the company might be able to buy those shares back from you at a lower price than what they're going for on the market, which usually caps the share price of those preferred. That callability also makes the effective yield stay within an attractive range because the share price of the preferred usually won't just go up in lock step with the company itself.

Now, normally if a company pays interest on its debt and pays dividends on its preferred or even if it just pays dividends on its common shares, the normal way to do that is from business [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profits]]. In other words, the company has an underlying business where they are selling a product and after all of their expenses for people and real estate and advertising, they are left over with more money than it costs them to run the business. So they have a [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] and they distribute some of that [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] to their shareholders.

The owners want to take advantage of the company being profitable and get paid a return on their investment. That is how normal dividends work. But today in the over financialized world that we live in, there are many things called dividends that I don't like to look at as dividends personally. For instance, there are ETFs like XYLD.

And I'm not picking on XYLD here. There are literally hundreds of these things that pay a very attractive dividend rate. This one in particular right now pays almost 10%. But you have to ask, how is the income for this dividend being produced? And for XYLD, this is the long-term chart.

That income is being produced by selling away the upside to the shares that it holds. That is what the covered call strategy does. You are allowing somebody else to pay you for the right to take the upside of the shares you own. Which means the total return of these funds over the long term is abysmal.

Even when you look at the total return chart, which includes all the dividends these funds have paid, this one specifically since inception has a total return of only 146% while the S&P 500 by itself has returned over 400%. And so unless the income is being produced from an underlying business being [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profitable]] and returning some of those [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profits]] to its shareholders, I don't like looking at it as a dividend as income. It's being produced in another way, which means there's risk involved. Sometimes that risk is the risk of losing out on the real upside potential of your holdings.

But sometimes in the case of Micro Strategy, that risk is just that the income evaporates one day. And that's because we know what Micro Strategy does with its business. The only thing it does, the vast majority of what it does is it raises money by issuing new debt or issuing new shares of its [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|common stock]] or issuing shares of a new preferred like Strike or Stride or Stretch. And it takes that new money that it's raised from investors or lenders and it uses the vast majority of that to buy [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]].

And then it takes some of it and uses it to pay the dividends on those preferred shares and pay the debt cost, the interest expense, the debt service payments. This is why people feel like it is very Ponziike because the only way it is paying out dividends and payments is by raising new money. And a lot of that new money that it's raising from things like its preferred, it's telling them, hey, we plan on paying you a dividend. Which in this case, personally, I wouldn't consider this a dividend because it is not income being produced by an underlying [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] from the business.

This is income that is just being taken from a percentage or a portion of new investor cash. Now, it is true that they take the vast majority of the money they raise and they put it into [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]]. And [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] historically has done one thing and that is go up. And if that continues to happen, Micro Strategy will be fine.

But if that doesn't continue to happen, if [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] enters a bare market or it stagnates for a long time and therefore Micro Strategy has an issue with raising new funds, it doesn't have the ability to continue to pay these dividends. Now, that fact is laid out very clearly in the issuing of these preferred. There's no fraud happening here. There are no guarantees or promises being made that cannot be fulfilled.

But some people are trying to say, "Hey, well, Micro Strategy has so much [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] and so much [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profits]] on their [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] that they never have to worry about it.
