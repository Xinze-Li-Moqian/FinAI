# The Market at All Time Highs - Don’t Make This Mistake

The market is trading at all-time highs again. And in fact, it seems like it always does. However, this time around, it seems like there is heightened fear because of fears of an AI bubble, [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Geopolitical Risk|geopolitical risks]], the dollar failing, the yen failing. There are a mountain of worries in front of us that are causing concern about investing right now.

Does it make any sense to buy the market when valuations seem so stretched, when prices seem so high? And is there any way to actually know? Well, the answer is obviously no. We cannot see the future, but we can see the past and that can give us an indication of what usually happens when similar circumstances are present. In other words, studying the past doesn't guarantee that we make the right decision for the future, but it can increase our odds of success.

So, while the market is at all-time highs, what does the past data say about investing? We're going to look at a couple of sets of data here. Number one, we're going to be looking at investing at all-time highs. Number two, we're going to be looking at things like dollar cost averaging versus lump sum investing.

And number three, we're going to be looking at investing with proper timing, bad timing versus not trying to time things at all. And the reason why we're looking at this is because when you are fearful, it is very likely that you do not make the right decision, if you make any decision at all. Our brains are wired for survival, to avoid risk. But the actions that we take to survive and avoid life-threatening risk are very different than the actions that are necessary to make money over the long-term investing.

And most often that's because the times when it seems like the worst time to invest when those risk flags are blaring in your brain saying to run away, many times those are the best times to invest where you make the most amount of money long term. Let me show you what I mean. Take a look at this chart which did a study going back to 1988. We have two colored bars here.

We've got the green bars and we've got the gray bars. The gray bars represent the average returns that you would have over a given time frame investing on any random day. Whereas the green bars represent only investing on days where new all-time highs are being made in the market. For example, if we were looking at the S&P 500 just over the last couple of months, this would mean you'd only be investing on days where new highs were made, like August 12th, August 13th, August 28th, September 10th.

You get the point. And you can see that while the shortterm time frames are roughly equal, 3 months you would have done better investing on any day versus just on new highs, 6 months were equal, one year you'd be doing better investing on new highs. Within a year, these are roughly the same. However, once you get to longerterm time frames, investing only on days where the market is making new all-time highs far outperforms investing on any day.

Now, this seems counterintuitive because when the market's making all-time highs, by definition, it's the most expensive. So, if you're only buying things when they're the most expensive, it seems like your overall performance would be worse, not better. But the reason why this is true is because the vast majority of the time, the market is making all-time highs. Which means if those are the days where you're buying, you are capitalizing on the trends when the market is rising.

Now, this is far from the ideal strategy. That is not the point of this data. The point is to dispel the fears that you automatically get by investing at all-time highs because you think it's never been this high before, it's got to go back down. That's not true.

Which brings us to the second set of data we're looking at, which is how well it works to try to time the market. Now, this study was done by Charles Schwab. And you can see we have five hypothetical characters here. We have Peter who had the best performance, Ashley who had the second best, Matthew who had the middle performance, Rosie who had the second worst, and Larry who had the absolute worst performance.

So what are the differences between what each of these five characters did? Well, I want you to guess. I'm going to tell you what the strategies were and then you try and guess which one of these characters used each strategy. One strategy that was used on this chart was dollar cost averaging which meant that they took a specific number of dollars and invested it each month.

Second strategy was lump sum investing. So instead of spreading out your investments over a year, you invest it all at the beginning of the year. The third strategy was trying to time the market which meant buying the dips and doing it successfully. The next approach was also trying to time the market by buying the dips but it was done unsuccessfully.

And then the other strategy was just fear, waiting in cash. So again, we have dollar cost averaging, lumpsum investing, good timing, bad timing, and cash. Now, if you need to pause the video to make your guesses, go ahead and do so. And now the results.

Coming in at the bottom with the absolute worst performance was Larry, who just kept his money in cash. Now, many people think, okay, well, this is obvious. If you want to make money investing, you have to invest. And if you stay in cash, you're going to have the worst performance.

But this is important because a lot of people think they're trying to time the market while they wait in cash. And what ends up happening is the market just keeps going for years and years and years and the crash they were waiting for never happens. And even when it does, like the market crashed 20% in April of 2025, they still think the market hasn't really crashed enough and so they still never invest at the bottom or during the crash that they were waiting for all along. And then when the market inevitably recovers and goes on to make all-time highs, they're even more set in stone about staying in cash until the real crash eventually happens later, which never ever comes.

And so, it's not that the people who stay in cash were ever intending to do so from the beginning. It's because the fear of loss prevented them from taking action when those opportunities presented themselves. And so, they end up staying in cash even despite the fact that they intended to buy the debt. But when it came, the fear never allowed them to invest.

Moral of the story is don't be Larry, who let his lizard brain guide his investing actions and missed out on the ability to generate wealth. Now, let's go over the next strategies. But before we do, I need you to look at this chart and notice the similarities in the performance between Peter, Ashley, Matthew, and Rosie. Even though Peter did outperform Rosie, you can see that it wasn't by a life-changing amount, which means that the strategies of good timing, bad timing, dollar cost averaging, and lumpsum investing, while they do differ, actually still end up being pretty similar.

And this is an important lesson that should dispel your fears enough to avoid being Larry because even with bad timing, which by the way was Rosie, she was trying to time the market and she ended up doing it very poorly, she still got invested. And even though she had worse performance than Matthew, Ashley, and Peter, it was still drastically better than staying in cash. Investing with bad timing beats not investing. All right, next up on the list, we have Matthew.

And his strategy was dollar cost averaging. So, the amount of money he was going to invest throughout the year, he did that throughout the year, even amounts each month. And you can see statistically that ends up beating bad timing which makes sense because you are buying more when it's lower, buying less when it's higher because you're allotting the exact same dollar amount. Now we get to Ashley and her strategy was actually lump sum investing.

So instead of spreading out the amount she was going to invest over the entire year, invested it all at the beginning of the year. The reason why that outperforms dollar cost averaging on average is because the market usually goes up, which means the earlier you invest, the higher the odds are that you invest at a much lower price. Dollar cost averaging on the way up means your overall cost is higher, not lower. Dollar cost averaging only works out better if the market's going down while you're investing.

So on average, Ashley, who lumpsum invests, beats Matthew, who dollar cost averages. And obviously, the best performance here is Peter, who tried to time the market and actually did it successfully. Now, I want you to know here that there is no right or wrong approach to this. If you can't sleep at night because you've lump summed everything into the market on January 1st and you're worried the market's about to crash, dollar cost averaging usually will be a little bit worse, but not always.

Sometimes will be better. So, if it helps you sleep at night and helps you stay invested, then for you, dollar cost averaging might be better than lump sum investing. And also, timing the market is not necessarily a bad thing. As you can see here, if you time the market right, you can pretty decently outperform everybody else.

And if you time the market poorly, guess what? It's not going to ruin you. And the more you do it, odds are the better you will get as long as you continue to learn and improve along the way. Now, I know everybody right now is thinking, "Well, that's all fine and dandy for past results, but right now we have a lot of things to worry about."

And you're absolutely right. We have AI bubble fears. We have [[Finance/12 Trade and Geopolitics/Geopolitical Risk|geopolitical risks]]. We have dollar fears. There's always something to worry about. And that's a good thing because once we get to the point where there is nothing to worry about and everybody feels like investing right now is the right decision, that means most people have invested, which means the buyers have dried up. We've gotten to the euphoria phase. That means the markets have probably topped at least temporarily.

You don't want euphoria. You want worry. That's why the old adage is that markets climb a wall of worry. Now obviously that doesn't mean that none of those concerns are justified. In fact, many of them are justified. And as a result, many people are also saying that it's not just price, it's valuations.

For example, if you look at mega caps right now, Apple, Amazon, Broadcom, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Nvidia, the PEG ratio is at the lowest place that it's been since about 2019. There was a brief point at the bottom of the bare market in 2022 when the PEG ratios were slightly lower, but we are nowhere near extreme territories.

And just in case you're curious, the PEG ratio is taking the price to earnings ratio and comparing that to the growth of those earnings. Now, I want to be clear, what I am not saying here is that now is a good time to buy. I am not saying that now is the best time to buy. I'm not saying that you should buy.

This isn't financial advice. I don't know you. I don't know your situation. So you sitting in 90 or 100% cash is not following in the footsteps of Warren Buffett who has less than a third of his assets in cash, but actually it's in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. The point is that historically speaking, the people who make the most money by investing in markets do it by buying great companies as early as they can and as often as they can.

Great timing can help, but it's absolutely not necessary.
