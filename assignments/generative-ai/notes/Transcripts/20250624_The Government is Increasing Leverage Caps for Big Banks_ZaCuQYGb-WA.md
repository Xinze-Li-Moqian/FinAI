# The Government is Increasing Leverage Caps for Big Banks

The plan to deregulate the banks that I've been warning about for a while now is finally here. Recently, the US announced its plans to ease capital rule, which limits banks treasury trades. Because right now, banks have what some would consider to be very tight restrictions on how many US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] they can purchase. In other words, how much money they can loan to the US government.

And now US bank regulators plan to reduce that key capital buffer by up to 1 and a half percentage points for the biggest lenders. This rule is the enhanced supplementary leverage ratio and it applies to banks like Chase, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley. It would take the requirement down from the current 5% to in between 3 and 1/2 and 4 1/2%. It would also reduce the requirements of the bank's subsidiaries to the same range down from the current 6%.

Now, changes like this are not unprecedented. We saw similar revisions back in 2018, and we saw drastic changes taking these requirements all the way down to 0% in 2020 and 2021. I'll talk about that more in just a moment. But the question is, why are they doing this now?

Well, it's because the federal government currently owes $36 trillion in debt. And so far in fiscal year 2025, just the interest payments on the debt have accounted for 14% of all the money the US government has spent in dollars. That has totaled $665 billion. And keep in mind that is just the net interest, not the total interest payments on the debt.

Total interest payments on the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] are nearing $1.2 trillion annually. And considering that the federal government's total income from [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxation]] was under $5 trillion in 2024, it means the US government is spending approximately 25% of every dollar it [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] from you just to pay interest on the debt. Let me say that again. For every dollar the US government takes in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] from your paycheck, 25% of that is going just to pay interest on the debt.

Now, part of the reason for that is because [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are so much higher than they were just a few years ago as a result of all of the crazy borrowing and spending the government has been doing. In fact, the last time [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] were this high were all the way back in 2007 at a time when the total [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] was under $9 trillion compared to today's 36 trillion. Now, we know that with US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], they are not an entirely controlled market. There is influence exerted on them by entities like the Fed, but most Treasury buying and selling happens from financial institutions outside of the government's control.

And just like anything that has buying and selling, the price is going to be determined by that supply in demand. This means that with [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] as high as they are, if the US government wants those rates to come down, they need more buyers of [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] to drive the price of [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] up, which pushes the rates lower. This is the inverse of the chart of something like TLT, which is an ETF that invests in [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. You can see that the price per share has fallen because the demand for [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] has fallen.

If more buyers come in to buy up more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], it would push the price of [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] back up, which would push the [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] or the yield back down. So, one of the main things the US government is trying to do right now in order to help out its massive debt burden or at least just the interest payments on the debt is it's trying to attract more dollars to be lent to the US government in the form of buying US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. This is why regulators are looking to lower the supplementary leverage ratio so that banks can purchase more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] considering the capital requirements.

easing these restrictions simply makes it easier for these banks to buy more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. Now, this is just step one. They will probably continue to ease these restrictions all the way down to zero because at the end of the day, this is just a band-aid. Tweaking how much a few banks can purchase of US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] does not change how much the government is borrowing, how much debt the government already has, how much the government is spending, and what the deficit is every single year.

All it does is it temporarily makes all of those other problems and issues a little bit less painful in the short run. It's like if you have a massive [[Concepts/Finance/Household Finance/Mortgages|mortgage]] and a home equity line of credit and you've got huge loans on your cars and you've maxed out your credit cards, it's not going to help you in the long run to get a little bit more debt in the form of like a personal loan from a bank. It might make things temporarily less painful for you because you have a little extra [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] that's not just going to pay off all of your debt or at least just the minimum payments, but it's not going to help out your situation in the long run just to have somebody else who's willing to lend you a little bit more money.

In fact, it's likely to just make things worse. Now, I said that they are likely to take this all the way down to zero. And even that is not without precedent. Back in the fallout of COVID, that is exactly what regulators did. the supplementary leverage ratio went all the way down to 0% which meant banks did not have to worry about any of those capital requirements and they could buy as many US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] as they wanted.

Problem with that is that was when [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] were basically at 0% which means banks gorged themselves on all of this US [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] that was paying next to nothing. Now, that's exactly what happened. Some [[Concepts/Finance/Banking and Credit/Bank Runs|bank runs]] started and some banks collapsed as a result of not having the money because the assets they were forced to sell in order to meet all those withdrawal requests were not worth as much as all of the withdrawal requests. And so the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] stepped in and they created something called the bank term funding program.

the BTFP, which basically said, "Hey banks, if you've got [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] that are worth less than what you paid for them, you can sell them to us and we'll buy them from you at full price. We'll pay for them what you paid for them, even though that's not what they're worth. The only requirement is you have to buy those [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] back from us within a year." Now, obviously, this meant that banks were no longer sitting on real unrealized losses because if you if you and everybody else at the bank went to go get your money out, that bank could sell their [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] to the Fed for full price and you wouldn't have to be worried about the bank collapsing, you could actually get your money.
