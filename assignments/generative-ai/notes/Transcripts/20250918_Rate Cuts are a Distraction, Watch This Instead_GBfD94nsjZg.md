# Rate Cuts are a Distraction, Watch This Instead

All eyes are on the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] policy, but that might just be a distraction from the main thing you should be paying attention to, which is their balance sheet. And this is because of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] third mandate that has more control over the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] market, at least according to Steven Moran, who cited in a congressional testimony that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] must pursue moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. Now, if you're sitting there wondering who the heck is Steven Moran, well, he's the newest member of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], just recently confirmed by the Senate in a vote 48 to 47.

He was confirmed just hours before the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] 2-day FOMC meeting began. So, with the highlight that Steven Moran put on the Fed's third mandate, what in the world is the Fed's third mandate? Well, we're all familiar with the dual mandate. And to be honest, I don't know why that phrase ever became popular.

Let's go back and look at the original text and you'll find there are not two mandates there. There are three. Right now, we are looking at a copy of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Act of 1977. We're going to scroll down on the first page to section 2A. The board of governors of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve System]] and the FOMC shall maintain longun growth of the monetary and credit aggregates commensurate with the economy's longun potential to increase production.

Why? so as to promote effectively the goals of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. Those are the three goals, the three things that Congress says to the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. This is the reason you exist, and this is why we allow you to continue to exist.

And this is now your job, what you are supposed to do and pursue. Maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. And it is that third one that is going to drive the next decade plus. Before we get into how that will happen though, before we get into how the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] moderates long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], I do want to touch on the initial opening sentence of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] Act of 1977.

That they are to maintain long-run growth of the monetary credit aggregates commensurate with the economy's long-run potential to increase production. Now in a hypothetical world that didn't exist. Imagine the number of dollars, the supply of money was exactly constant. It couldn't change. It couldn't increase or decrease.

In this world, we are creating more houses. We are creating more food. We are creating more energy efficiency. We are creating more things, more stuff. There is innovation everywhere, which means you'd have the same amount of money chasing more stuff, more goods and services, more wealth.

The necessary result of that would be that prices would have to adjust downwards. Just like if you have more money, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], money printing, chasing the same amount of goods and services, you have [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] like we saw in 2020 2021. The opposite is true as well. If you have the same amount of money chasing more goods and services, you have [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Deflation|deflation]].

But the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is tasked specifically with maintaining the growth of the monetary and credit aggregates commensurate with the economy's long-run potential to increase production. In other words, the monetary and credit aggregates, the money supply, the growth of spending power in the economy should grow roughly at the same rate as the increase in production. In a world without intervention, we would see prices declining and the cost of living getting cheaper over time and the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] of your money going up. But because of the intervention that Congress has told the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to do to your money, you lose out on those cost savings.

You lose out on getting wealthier over time. That [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] is siphoned away from you to the government, even if prices stay the same. But just to make sure they're doing a good enough job at that, stable prices means 2%. [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|Inflation]], which means prices aren't even staying the same for you.

They're going up. This is why the Fed exists to transfer [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] as efficiently as possible from the entire economy to the government. Okay, so now let's talk about how they do that with moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. The Trump administration and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessant have been pushing the importance of longdated rates going down for a long time, specifically for economic growth and things like home ownership.

Long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] would be things like the 10-year yield or longer now. The long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] matter a lot more to the economy than the short-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. Yes, if short-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] go up or down, that affects your high yield savings account. that affects the yield that you get on your money market funds, but it doesn't have that much to do with overall production and long-term investing in the United States.

The 10-year yield is a much better benchmark. The reason why is because if I, just a normal person, have money that I want to put to work, but I want to put it in something really safe, I'm going to consider [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] because they are looked at as the risk-free rate. So, I could give my money to the US government for 10 years and get 4% per year on that. Now, there's other people that want to borrow my money other than the US government.

So, I could look at Bank of America. I could look at Amazon. I could look at Apple. I could look at my friends. I could look at somebody who wants to borrow for a [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] or a car. And if they all also want to borrow from me at 4%, of course, I'm not going to give them my money.

They don't have the power of violence and taxing in order to collect my money in a worst case scenario. They might default. The US government at 4% is never going to default. At the end of the day, even if they can't [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|tax]] it, they can still print it.

So, I know I'm going to get the 4% on my money for the next 10 years if I give it to the government. Which means if I am going to give my money, lend it to somebody else for the next 10 years, I'm going to need a higher rate than 4%. Maybe 5%, maybe 6%, who knows? And given the fact that [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] are usually refinanced or paid off within 10 years, [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]] are creeping up next to 10 years, business loans, commercial real estate, all these loans have an average maturity somewhere around 10 years.

The 10-year yield is a fantastic benchmark for determining what [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] are for the rest of the economy. In other words, they set a floor and any risk added on top will increase yields or [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. So now imagine that the US government needs to borrow so much using 10-year [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] only that it drives [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] all the way up to 9 or 10% where they've been before. That means all other borrowing in the economy would have to again be higher than that, maybe 11, 12, 13%.

Because if I can get a risk-free 10% from the US government for 10 years, I'm going to demand a way higher [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] from everybody else. On the other hand, if yields on the 10-year drop to 3% or 2 1/2% or lower, then there's room for [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] across the rest of the economy to drop as well. It won't necessarily push them lower, but it could allow for them to go lower because if I can only get 2.5% from the US government, that might not be good enough for me. I might choose the extra risk for the extra return.

that frees up a bunch of capital, a bunch of lending to go and be productive in the actual economy instead of just going to the government. All this extra demand to lend to the private economy can push yields across the economy lower. But here's the thing, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] does not control the 10-year. They only control overnight rates between banks.

So, when the Fed cuts rates, it doesn't necessarily have any influence on things like the 10-year, which is why last time they cut rates, which was in September of 2024, and then they ended cutting rates, January 2025, it actually made the 10-year yield rise. It went from 3.6% all the way up to 4.8%. Now, this might seem counterintuitive, but it's just about [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]]. When the Fed cuts overnight [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], that makes borrowing for the government easier.

As long as they're borrowing short-term debt, it makes it cheaper. They're able to borrow and spend more money into existence. That increases the money supply. That's more money chasing the same amount of goods and services. That makes prices go up. And if prices are going to go up, lenders across all other forms of debt are going to demand a higher [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] to compensate themselves for the loss of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]].

Which is why when the Fed cut rates starting last September, it made other forms of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] actually get more expensive. So, the Fed cutting [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] isn't going to cut it. Pun intended. Which brings the question, how do they actually maintain moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] according to their third mandate?

The answer is their balance sheet. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessant has talked about how he doesn't like the negative effects from the Fed doing [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]], although he said that it's still okay in true emergencies. Here's the thing, the [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|sovereign debt]] crisis would probably be considered a true emergency by the Treasury Secretary. So, option one is that we see the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] resume [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] and their balance sheet starts increasing again.

You can think of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Bank Balance Sheets|Federal Reserve balance sheet]] like their brokerage account. They buy assets. The Fed buys [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities and [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. These are forms of debt. And when this debt gets paid back, they take some of those dollars that were paid back to them and buy new debt.

In other words, roll it over, loan it back out into the economy. But when that balance sheet is declining, that means some of those dollars that are getting paid back to them, they are not rolling that over and reinvesting it. Those dollars are just ceasing to exist, which means the size of their balance sheet goes down. In other time periods when the balance sheet is rising, it means they are [[Finance/15 Monetary History/Money Creation and Destruction|creating money]], printing money into existence to go out into the economy, into the market and buy up assets.

They're lending cash into existence. And so if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is tasked with maintaining moderate long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], and that gets interpreted as the 10-year yield going down, the easiest way to accomplish this would be for the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to go out into the open market and just start buying up 10-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] with newly printed money. that drives the price of 10-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] up, which drives the yield lower. But that's not the only option.

The Treasury itself could borrow at the short end with T bills even more and use that extra borrowing to do a bunch of buybacks for longerdated [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] like 10-year because right now forever, the government always spends everything that it [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]], spends even more, and borrows the difference. That's called running a deficit. That's why we have such a large [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]]. And so this proposes that they would borrow even more at the short end, like six month T bills or one-year T bills, and use that extra money they're borrowing in order to pay back buy back longerterm [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]].

This would be like you opening up some new credit cards, getting a cash advance in order to pay off a longer term personal loan. Your total debt would stay the same. You're just transferring some of that longerterm debt into shorter term debt. This would be helpful in the short run.

and be like a band-aid for the economy because the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] cutting rates does influence things like T bills, which is why those short-term rates have dropped because they are more influenced by the Fed cutting rates. There is more demand for Ta bills from financial institutions and so the government does have the ability to issue more debt at the short end of the curve. They can use that extra borrowing and pay off some of that longerterm debt. The problem is this puts the US government in a pickle over the long term because they basically are just putting their entire [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] into short-term T bills that constantly roll over every 6 to 12 months.

They're constantly rolling over $30 trillion worth of debt. And if short-term rates ever do go up as a result of that, that's an instant added cost on the entire [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]]. So, it's a band-aid in the short term, dangerous for the government in the long term, but could provide a boost for the economy in the meantime because those 10-year yields going lower would provide stimulus for economic activity, which means that we are likely to get some sort of a return to the playbook from 1942 through 1951 when the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] engaged in [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control.

The Fed and the Treasury worked together to make sure that the Treasury could borrow at that longer term end of the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] at a rate that was lower than the rate of [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. In other words, a negative real rate. And because this was enforced on the economy, it wasn't something that the free market did to itself. It allowed the government to offload the cost of its debt onto the economy.

This was very effective at that time because things like gold ownership were outlawed. [[Finance/07 Crypto and Digital Money/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] didn't exist. It was very difficult for the average person to invest in [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] and it was considered patriotic to buy things like US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasury bonds]]. But the anticipation of policies like this are why we are seeing dramatic rises in things that are harder assets like [[Finance/07 Crypto and Digital Money/Bitcoin|Bitcoin]] and gold and [[Finance/08 Gold Silver and Commodities/Silver|silver]] and [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] and real estate.
