# It’s Not the Fed Who Actually Controls Interest Rates

Despite what you may have heard, it is not the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] who actually controls [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. There are two main things the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] controls in order to enact its [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]]. And the first one is this, which is the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]]. And the second is this, which is the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] balance sheet.

So despite all the excitement or the hope that many people are experiencing right now that [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgage Interest Rates|mortgage rates]] will be coming down soon, [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loan]] rates will be coming down soon, credit card rates will be coming down soon or even US Treasury rates will be coming down soon. The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] does not control these, which means by the Fed lowering rates, it may not have any impact or may not have as much impact on your [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] as you're hoping for. Why is that, though? and most importantly, what does influence those [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] the most?

First, let's look at what they actually do control. Starting with this one, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]]. This is what people are talking about when they say the Fed is raising or lowering [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. You can see from about the 40s through the 80s, the Fed was on average raising rates.

And then from 1980 through about 2020, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] was on average lowering rates. If we zoom in just on what has happened since 2022 when the Feds started raising [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], they then held them steady and then since have lowered them slightly. The effective [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] right now is 4.22%. And this [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] is a range that they try and make sure the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] stays between.

This is not an [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that matters for you and I. This is the rate that banks get paid on their cash when they lend to each other in overnight markets. That's it. has nothing to do with T- billills or [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] or [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]] or [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] even. And in order to make sure that this rate stays in between the range that they want it to, they will directly intervene in this overnight market.

And if banks place the reserves with the Fed themselves, the Fed will just pay that [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] to the banks by printing it into existence themselves. We are going to come back to this later because it's important, but currently there's about $3.2 $2 trillion in [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]] that banks are just holding at the Fed and getting the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Fed funds rate]] directly from the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] themselves. So, this is a market that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just outright controls. But it is not the only tool the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] uses for [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] and sometimes it's not the most effective either.

That brings us to the second most powerful tool the Fed uses, which is their balance sheet. You can see currently the Fed has a balance sheet worth about $6.5 trillion, significantly lower than where it was when it peaked in 2022 at almost $9 trillion. You can think of the Fed's balance sheet like a brokerage account. They [[Finance/15 Monetary History/Money Creation and Destruction|create money]] out of thin air, use that new money to buy assets from the open market like US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgage]] back securities and place those assets on their balance sheet.

When that accumulation is happening, in other words, they are doing buying of assets. Their balance sheet is growing. During times like this, it is called [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]]. Now, [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] is a very important way to describe what they are doing. So, remember that because it's quantitative, not qualitative.

We're going to come back to that in a moment. Now, the opposite is true as well. When the balance sheet is declining, they're doing something called [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|quantitative tightening]], which means assets are leaving their balance sheet. This happens from general bleed off when assets get paid back but they do it in an orderly fashion.

So sometimes they do have to roll over, sometimes they have to buy more but overall the assets on their balance sheet are declining. This means cash is being extracted, liquidity reserves being extracted from the financial system since it goes back to the Fed itself. That money just gets destroyed. It ceases to exist.

So [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] add cash take out assets. [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|QT]] both the assets and the cash cease to exist. Now, that's important because it's not that these assets are just getting sold back and then into the market being traded around between other people. This is debt that's getting paid off.

Dollars are lent into existence, which means when that debt gets paid off, both the asset and the money ceases to exist. The wonders of the fiat financial system. Now, depending on the scale in which they are doing [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] or tightening, this can have huge impacts on the market, but again, it's not directly controlling [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. For example, if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] decides to go out there and buy a hundred billion dollars worth of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] over the course of a couple of years, it's not really going to move the markets at all.

Yes, it's a little extra buying pressure, so prices of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] will be a little bit higher than they would be otherwise, but not much. On the flip side, though, when the Fed goes out and buys $5 trillion worth of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] from 2020 through 2022, that's going to have a huge impact on the market, and that's going to push [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] down. That's going to have a big impact on lifting the prices of those [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. But keep in mind here, this is called [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] because they're making a decision based on how many [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] to buy or sell, how much liquidity to inject or extract from the market.

They are not saying we want [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for this specific [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] to be exactly here and so we will buy or sell whatever is necessary to make that happen. They're just saying, "Hey, we're going to buy a trillion dollars worth of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] or a billion dollars worth of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] or sell a hundred billion worth of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and the rest of the market forces are also going to be at play and will determine what the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] or the yield ends up being." Now, if you notice this chart, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has been doing [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|quantitative tightening]] for a couple of years now.

Their balance sheet has been declining, which has been sucking liquidity out of the financial system. Over that time period, on average, [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] have risen. But recently, rates have been falling. even the 10-year Treasury yield is much lower than where it was a couple of months ago.

This despite the fact that their balance sheet is still declining over that period of time. So, if they're extracting liquidity, how is it that rates are falling? Especially because we just went over the fact that them cutting [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] is only cutting [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] for what banks get paid on their overnight reserves. Well, there's two things at play here.

One of them is front running, and the other one is what's known as the crowding out effect. To understand this first, let's take a look at this chart which is called the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. Now, the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility is where banks can give extra cash to the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] in exchange for collateral that they need and they get paid a nice little [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] on it while the cash sits there. If that sounds similar to [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|bank reserves]] getting paid an overnight [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balance]] at the Fed, you're right, it does.

But after all the money printing that took place in 2020 and 2021, banks needed a place to park the cash and get the collateral they needed. And that all flooded into the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility because the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] was paying a risk-free [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] of 5% to banks just to hold all this cash with the Fed. This meant that all of that cash was literally parked, hidden, taken, shielded, held on to by the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] on purpose because the Fed didn't want all of that cash being flooded out into the marketplace.

The Fed was trying to raise [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. The Fed is trying to tighten. The Fed was trying to make sure [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] didn't get out of control. So, they were telling banks, "Hey, don't go lend this out to people for cheap. Keep it here and we will reward you for making sure nobody else can get access to this money.

Sounds a little twisted. It is. Welcome to the world of the fiat financial system. This is what's known as the crowding out effect. Usually we talk about it from the standpoint of like [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], but it stands in this case as well. If the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] can print money into existence and they're saying risk-free, we'll give you 5% on your money, then financial institutions are going to do a lot of that.

They're not going to put that money out in the marketplace for, you know, 6% or 7% and take on a ton of extra risk. And they're definitely got not going to take on extra risk to loan that money out to somebody else for less than 5%. But the opposite of this is also true. When the Fed does start to lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], that means that you no longer have access to that really high [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] on risk-free money anymore.

So, you got to take that and you got to deploy it elsewhere. When you push this to its extreme, let's say you can't get anything over a half a percent from the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], all of the cash leaves that and goes out to find somewhere else to deploy it. Which means now you have a lot of lenders competing for borrowers, which pushes [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] across the curve down. And that's part of the reason why we've seen long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] start to fall recently because of frontr running this with financial institutions largely anticipating the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] lowering [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] over the next year or so.

Big money wants to get in front of that and lock in some of the high rates. Now, for example, right now you can get tea bills for over 4% when you're looking at 4 weeks or 6 weeks, but go out to like a year and you're looking at like 3 and 12%. You might think, well, why wouldn't you just put it in a four-week or a 6 week T bill and just continue to roll it over? It's because the market expects that by the time you get to a half a year from now or a full year from now, the Fed will have lowered [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]].

And so big money is willing to lock up their funds for a longer period of time right now, which puts a bid on Treasury prices, which pushes yields lower because they're willing to lock their money up for a lower [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] right now with the anticipation that over the course of that entire lifetime of that [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] that that will actually end up being a higher yield for them than if they took the short-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] and just had to completely continually roll them over. If that doesn't make sense, let's say you could get 10% in the first year, but 1% every year after that for like 50 years.

Or you could get 5% every single year, lock that in for 50 years. Clearly, it would be better to get 5% every year, lock that in for 50 years, than to get a higher rate in the beginning, and then a much lower rate for the rest of the term. That's what's going on here. But we have another big problem at play, and that is a $38 trillion elephant in the room.

This is why the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] will have to start doing [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] again because the market will need somebody else to come in and be the buyer of these [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. The reason why the market will need this is because as the US government borrows and spends, that is new money coming into existence. That is how debt works in a fiat financial system. When you go and borrow money, you are not transferring dollars from one bank account to another bank account.

Dollars are lent into existence. So when you have a government that is spending $2 trillion every year, more than they are taking in [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Taxation|taxes]], that means there's $2 trillion a year of new money that didn't exist before that is being added to the money supply. This bids up the prices of goods and services. And within a short period of time, I think this will become clear to the market that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] will be taking back off again as a result of this [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Government Spending|government spending]] that cannot do anything but spiral further out of control.

And buyers are not going to want to hold on to [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] at 3 to 4% anymore. They'll only be willing to lend to the government if they can get a higher rate of return, something that at least compensates them for the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] that is expected during that time period. Now, the reason why I think this is going to happen is because this is exactly what happened last time the US hit 120% debt to GDP ratio.
