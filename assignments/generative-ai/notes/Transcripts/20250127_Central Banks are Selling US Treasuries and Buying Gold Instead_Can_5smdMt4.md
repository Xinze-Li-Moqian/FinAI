# Central Banks are Selling US Treasuries and Buying Gold Instead

China, Japan, and the United States are all selling US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] and [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] around the world are buying gold instead. This [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] activity is having impacts on the market and it will continue to have an impact on the market for years if not decades to come. So, the first big seller of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], we cannot ignore the United States Treasury themselves. Anytime the US government borrows money, that is the same thing as them selling or issuing a new Treasury.

The [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] is currently above $36 trillion and with the deficit already at $710 billion since October of 2024, the US government is borrowing like never before, which means it is selling [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] like never before. But, we also can't forget about the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] in the United States. This is their balance sheet and you can see when this chart goes down, that is the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] selling [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] that it has previously bought. This means what has been happening for the last couple of years is the opposite of [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]].

When [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] is happening, [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]], the Fed is printing money to buy up US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]]. That drives the price up, that drives [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] down. Instead, what has been happening for the last couple of years is the opposite. The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has been selling, very slowly, [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] off of their balance sheet, which has the opposite impact of money printing.

All else being equal, this would decrease the money supply. Now, we know all else is not equal. Many financial institutions, like banks, are doing the opposite. They are lending money into existence and they are buying US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]]. But, the United States is not the only player in town here.

We are also going to need to look at the other two very large players, China, Japan. China and Japan have both been selling US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] at near record paces. This is a big deal because China and Japan are the biggest foreign holders of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]]. Now, according to the US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] internal data, Japan and China, as of November of 2024, are still the largest foreign holders of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], but if they continue selling, they could lose that top spot soon considering the UK is not far behind China.

So, China, Japan, and the Fed are all actively decreasing their exposure to US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] with China about to drop below the UK in terms of the total [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] it holds. Now, you might look at how many [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] the Fed owns and just by looking at their total balance sheet, you might assume that they have a very large, historically speaking, balance of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]]. But, if instead we look at this as a percent of the total debt that is available, we can see that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] holding of US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] is back down to very close to average levels.

The Fed owns about 15% of the US government's debt that is held by the public. And while that percentage has been pretty volatile, ranging from 7% up to 25% over the last two decades, being around 15% where it is right now is much closer to historical norms going back to 2003. This is one of the reasons why [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] yields have continued to rise over the past few years because US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] are being sold by everyone to a much larger degree than they are being purchased. We are seeing US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] be actively replaced around the world, especially on [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Bank Balance Sheets|central bank balance sheets]], and it is being replaced by gold.

This is a chart that shows the monthly reported [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] activity in terms of purchases or sales of gold. Let me explain what you're looking at here. Across the middle, there is the zero line. Any blue bar that shows up above the zero line represents a purchase and any purple bar that shows up below the zero line represents a sale.

Together, we can net these out to see total net purchases versus net sales and come up with the black line that jumps around from bar to bar. And you can see that over the past two years, every single month outside of just a couple of months in 2023, net purchases by [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central banks]] have outpaced net sales. This is the main reason why the price of gold has risen so dramatically over the last couple of years as well. In fact, in just the last one year, the price of gold is up about 35%.

Now, many times the price of gold will go up because there is large purchasing of gold in anticipation of future [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], future money printing to handle debt crises by sovereign governments. Now, this time, that is certainly one of the reasons why we are seeing gold be purchased at the pace that it's being purchased right now. But, another reason is simply because it is a safe haven asset that maintains its [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] and it has done so for thousands of years. It represents an asset that is not somebody else's liability and in times of rising geopolitical tensions and uncertainty about the foundation of the global financial system, in other words, US debt, owning gold becomes less of a luxury and more of a necessity.

Now, I'm not making the case that any country anywhere in the world is going to make any sort of a successful pushback towards some sort of a [[Finance/15 Monetary History/Gold Standard|gold standard]] anytime soon. But, what I am saying is that as a savings account, nothing has beat gold for thousands of years and as a way to preserve your [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]], it is still the king. Now, the fact that everybody is selling US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] and yields are rising is probably going to have an impact on other things like the stock market as well. If you take a look at this very interesting chart, we are seeing two lines.

The blue line is the earnings yield of the S&P 500. The pink line is the yield on the US 10-year Treasury. So, the earnings yield is the percentage [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] that companies that are part of the S&P 500 generate. we take a look at these two yields compared to each other, we notice something very interesting.

There was very little correlation between US Treasury yields and the yield of the S&P 500 from the early 1900s up until the 1960s. A lot of that had to do with the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control as well as the fallout from the Great Depression and the World Wars. However, once we get to about the '60s, we can see a fairly tight correlation between the 10-year yield and the S&P 500's earnings yield. They rose pretty much in lockstep from the '60s until the end of the '70s and then have fallen pretty much in lockstep for the last 40 years.

But, if we zoom in here recently, we can see that there has been a dramatic rise in the yield of the 10-year Treasury that has not yet been followed by a dramatic rise in the S&P 500's earnings yield. Now, this could just be noise. But, if you think about the implication of what this chart is showing you, it would make sense to see upwards in the earnings yield of the S&P 500. Because if I invest $100 in the S&P 500 right now, I'm going to get back about 3%, a little over 3% in terms of the [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] that those companies are going to generate for me.

Now, as just a shareholder, I'm not going to have any influence over whether all of those [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profits]] will be passed back to me or not. But, what is true is that if you look at investing in the US 10-year Treasury right now, you'll get about 4.6% back on your money guaranteed for the next 10 years, which you cannot say about the S&P 500 because [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] necessarily have risk inherent to them that US [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] do not. And so, in a very real sense right now, you are paying a lot more for income by investing in the S&P 500 than you are by giving it to the US government.

Now, I'm not making the case that that is a good bet, but what I am saying is that many people will think that is a good bet. This is the crowding out effect. Imagine if the US government was offering 20% on its [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. Everybody would sell all of their [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] and just give their money to the US government instead because that's guaranteed, the market is not.

And until the market, companies in the stock market, were offering dividend yields of 10, 15, 20, 25, 30%, it would be very hard to compete with the US government. Now, given the fact that we are in the next phase of a long-term debt cycle, that the next decade or two decades will probably look very similar to how it looked from 1940 through 1980, very different than what we've experienced for the last 40 years with falling [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] and falling [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and growing debt. Instead, what we're probably going to see is a very long and extended inflationary [[Finance/05 Banking and Systemic Risk/Deleveraging|deleveraging]] where the US government, individuals, and institutions have to deleverage, happens through [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] as [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] and prices continue to rise, which means in the short term we are very likely to see some volatility, but also reversions to the mean.

And when you look at charts like this, it would imply that somehow in some way, we should expect the [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profits]] of the companies inside the S&P 500 to play catch-up. And that can happen from [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profits]] increasing, but it can also happen from the stock prices themselves going down.
