# You're A Day Trader Now (everyone is)

Well, it looks like you and everybody you know, we are all day traders now. Or if everyone is a day trader, does that actually mean nobody is? We go back in time to the very beginning of electronic trading, which obviously led to the dotcom bubble and subsequent burst. This was a revolutionary technology at the time.

I mean, imagine what you would have had to do before this. If you wanted to place a trade, you would have to call your broker on the phone, mail a check, and then wait for the check to get there for those funds to clear, and you would hope that the market hadn't moved in the meantime. This is why settlement times were so long on [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]]. And if you wanted to do something faster, maybe you'd go and see your broker in person.

This meant many orders were routed extremely slowly. They were filled on a not-held basis, meaning the broker was not held by you to a time or a price. They would fill the orders at their discretion. As you might imagine, this was extremely inefficient.

It was extremely costly. And the new ability to sit down in your home office, hop on a computer, wait for that dialup to spin up and get you your internet connection, and place a tray directly from your own computer in the comfort of your own living room was extremely popular. So popular in fact that everybody wanted to become a trader. But unfortunately the technology at that time was just not good enough to support everybody being a trader.

There were too many orders coming in from too many people trying to trade buying and selling [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] throughout the day that they just couldn't handle the order flow. And so starting in 2001, regulators introduced a $25,000 threshold if you wanted to day trade. Now, the problem with this is they had to come up with a definition of what a day trade was. And they also had to come up with a definition of what a pattern day trader was.

And if you didn't meet the threshold of $25,000 minimum in your account, you could not day trade past a certain number of times. that would get you labeled as a pattern day trader. And the rule they landed on and the definitions were that if you made more than three day trades in a rolling 5-day period, that would label you a pattern day trader. A day trade was defined as opening and closing the exact same position in the exact same day.

Which meant if you shorted a stock and then closed out some of that position or all that position, that's one day trade. If you went long a stock and then closed out some or all of that position in the same day, that's a day trade. If you do that more than three times in a rolling five-day period, you are now a pattern day trader. Which means you do two of them on Friday, you can only do one of them on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and if you do any more, then you're going to be labeled a pattern day trader.

If you have less than $25,000 in your account when you get flagged as a pattern day trader, you can no longer do any trading until you get your account above 25,000, even if you did it on accident. So after that point, once you're a pattern day trader, if your account ever starts the day below $25,000 and you're limited only to liquidating trades, at that point for somebody who is nowhere near the possibility of getting their account up to 25,000, they're restricted to converting their account over to a cash account and only using cash rules, which means you have to use settled cash only.

This means that since 2001, anybody trading with under $25,000 in their account cannot perform more than three day trades or if they want to, they're restricted to only being able to use the settled cash. Which means if you're using, let's say, $1,000 at a time for each trade, you've got $20,000 in your account, you could only do 20 trades until all of that cash from those trades goes through and settles. Does that sound complicated? It is complicated.

And guess what? For decades now, this rule has been completely obsolete. The technology rapidly improved to the point where it could have supported everybody and their mother-in-law being a day trader for the last 15, 20 plus years. And if you're thinking, well, this rule was probably good because it prevented people with no money from doing extra risky trading.

And certainly there is the possibility that somebody who shouldn't have been taking excess risk was prevented from taking excess risk because of these rules. But there's always a cost. And you have to also consider the fact that some people intentionally decided not to close out a trade that they would have closed out otherwise because they didn't want to get labeled as a pattern day trader and they suffered more losses than they would have otherwise if they would have closed out the trade. So, ironically, the pattern day trader rules have caused many traders to lose more money and take on more risk and stopped them from d-risking because they didn't want to get labeled a pattern day trader.

Well, thankfully, the SEC has finally eliminated the $25,000 pattern day trading rule. So the next question is if the PDT rule is eliminated, what are the new rules?

Can anybody just trade any amount they want? And the answer is almost kind of. Which is why you get headlines like this where people are saying that the relaxation of day trading rules is opening the door to yolo trading and higher risk. Now to be honest, anybody that was going to be yolo trading and increasing their risk was not being held back by pattern day trading rules.

They were still making unnecessarily risky trades and just maybe being prevented from closing them out as soon as they should have. But now, as long as you have over $2,000 in your account, your account can be a margin account. That part has not changed. Which means, as long as you have over $2,000 in your account, you can place as many day trades as you want, do as many round trips as you want in a single day, and never have to worry about it.

Let's say you've got a $5,000 account. You spend $5,000 on shares of Apple, and you sell those shares of Apple a couple minutes later. You do that again four dozen times throughout the rest of the day. you are completely fine. No ramifications other than the [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profits]] or the losses you might incur as a result of that trading.

Now, there are still rules regarding the margin requirements that you have if you incur margin balances in your trading. For example, as a day trader, you have access to purchase way more than just your cash would allow you to purchase if you're going to close out that trade today. Usually, it's four times your starting cash balance. which means if you start off the day with five grand in your account, you could technically make a purchase of $20,000.

Now, that's called day trade buying power, which means that you do have to close out that trade on the same day because if you want to hold a margin balance overnight, the amount you're able to use for purchase is much smaller than your day trade buying power. And so, if you do have intraday margin deficits by using day trade buying power and going too low and you violate those rules multiple times, you can face freezes on your account. The same rules that used to apply to people who had over $25,000 in their account now apply to everybody who gets a margin account, which is 2,000 or more.
