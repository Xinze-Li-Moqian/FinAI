# Is Private Credit About to Collapse the Economy?

We need to talk about private credit. Headlines are blowing up right now. BlackRock has just blocked withdrawals from its private credit fund. Blue Owl just tipped a UK lender into insolvency. JP Morgan is tightening lending to private credit. Morgan Stanley is now saying that default rates are going to reach 8% and [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]] with private credit exposure like KKR are falling off a cliff.

Blue Owl has taken a nose dive. Ares is collapsing. And when you hear of risky loans that are being packaged to investors bleeding over into losses in the overall stock market, it makes sense. You start to think this might be the great financial crisis 2.0.

So, what even is private credit? Who is investing in this garbage in the first place? Why is it blowing up right now? And most importantly for you and I, what kind of impacts might this have on the overall economy and the market? Well, for an overly simplistic origin story, we go back before the Great Financial Crisis.

And if you were a business and you needed a loan, you would go to the people who you normally would go to for a loan, the bank. But as we all know, banks got into a little bit of trouble after what they did leading up to the financial crisis and they were bailed out on the taxpayers' back. And so, because of that, people were justifiably out for blood and wanted to make sure banks couldn't do anything like that again. So, the politicians swooped in and regulated the crap out of the banking industry in the hopes that all of these new rules would stop banks from making the same mistakes in the future.

The only problem is, you have politicians doing this for political reasons and not necessarily doing things that make the most sense if you consider the consequences two or three or four steps down the line. They're legislators, which means all they do is make laws and often times are ignorant of economic reality. As they say, ignorance is 9/10 of the law. And one of these unforeseen consequences about putting all of these rules and regulations about what banks can lend money for and how much money they can lend to certain things meant that the appetite for borrowing would have to get fulfilled from somewhere else.

Because banks could no longer fulfill one of the main functions in the private economy that they always had, and that is lending to businesses. That doesn't mean demand for borrowing went away, it just means that demand for borrowing went somewhere else. And that somewhere else that they turned to we now call private credit, and some people call it the shadow banking system. It is simply non-bank lenders.

And the most common entity or form of a non-bank lender is going to be an investment company. I'm going to approach you and your buddies and say, "Hey, give me some of your money. I'm going to take it, put it together into a fund. I'm going to go turn around, and I'm going to loan that money to a bunch of businesses."

And the great thing is these businesses can no longer go to the banks and borrow money at normal rates. They have to come to us cuz we're the only ones who can lend to them, and we can charge a premium, which means we get a way better return than we would on things like [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] or corporate [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. Now, private credit didn't just explode on the scene from the start. It slowly grew over time to the point where it is right now.

Part of that was due to the really great returns that investors could get by investing in private credit. Typically, if you want to make decent returns like 9, 10, 11%, you're forced to invest in [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|equities]]. If you want something less volatile, considered safer, you can invest in [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]] like US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], and then you're looking at 3, 4, 5%. Or if you want to go with corporate [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]], maybe you're looking at 1% more.

But private credit offered a way for investors to be able to get equity-like returns, 9, 10, 11%, but with the low risk associated with debt, the lack of volatility that you would otherwise have with [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|equities]]. In other words, investors were attracted to something that looked like low-risk, high returns. Now, from the borrower's perspective, you might be thinking, "Why in the world would anybody borrow here?" But, it's because there's a large group of businesses who are not yet at the point where they can just issue [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]] to the public like Apple or Microsoft or Meta or any of the massive, large corporations.

But, they're too big to go to their local bank or get an SBA loan. And so, if they want to finance growth and more production, they're going to have to secure debt from somebody else. That's why they turn to private credit. And for a long time, it made sense.

Because when you look at the yield on the 10-year Treasury, you can see that following the Great Financial Crisis, it was regularly in the 2% range. Yes, sometimes it got up to 3%, but other times it got as low as 0.5%. And so, if I was a lender and I wanted to make any decent return on my money, but I didn't want to invest in [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Stocks|stocks]], I really wasn't going to be able to go after US [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], and I'm not going to be able to get that much of a premium going for corporate [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]].

But, a good rule of thumb for what you can get in private credit is about 5% higher than the risk-free rate. So, if I can invest in 10-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] at 2%, then I can invest in private credit and get 6%. That's a great deal for me as an investor because that is triple the income that I would have otherwise. And as a borrower, 6% is not astronomical.

It's not hard money lending. It's not something crazy. It's something that I can afford. When I run the numbers, I can pay my 6% and I can still finance production and grow the business. But, then 2020 happened, and you can see what we all remember, the Fed crushed [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] across the curve, the 10-year went down below 1%.

We entered an age of ZIRP and [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]], and that forced a couple of things to happen. Number one, there's now no return in traditional [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bonds]]. And so, money across the board is seeking places where it can make a better return very aggressively. Number two, because of all the money printing, there's a lot more money seeking a great return.

And so, post-2020, there is a flood of money coming into private credit, chasing those higher returns and that seemingly low risk. But, this presents a new problem and that's that there is more money that is chasing private credit than there are borrowers with the appetite to borrow. And so, these private credit funds that were being built and the money was coming in from new investors were being packaged up and they were being sold to new investors paying out earlier investors making them whole, which is just spreading the story about how great private credit is.

Meanwhile, they have to chase worse and worse borrowers just trying to get debt to fill up these funds. And then, [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] stuck around longer than policy makers thought it would and [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] rose. Following ZIRP and [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]], the Fed started raising [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] to try and get a handle on the [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] that they unleashed and you can see the 10-year Treasury went all the way up to 5% before stabilizing and moving sideways to where it is right now. And many people thought that by now [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] would have moved back down, but obviously they have not done that.

As of the time of this recording, the 10-year yield is still above 4%. Now, you might be thinking, if there is a fund that's designed to pay out a high [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]], then wouldn't it be better if [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] went up because then that fund could pay out an even higher rate? No.
