# Even Blackrock has Abandoned the 60/40 Portfolio

Are you invested in the standard 60/40 portfolio? If so, you might be leaving gains on the table or even worse, exposing yourself to risk of major loss over the coming years and decades. In fact, even Black Rockck has now moved away from the standard 60/40 portfolio advice, saying that investors can [[Finance/16 Economic Reasoning/Profit|profit]] from diversifying beyond it. So, what is the standard 60/40 portfolio?

This is considered traditional financial advice, even though historically it's actually relatively new. And it says that you should have a mix of both [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] and [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. The younger you are, the more [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]] you have and the fewer [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]. And as you get older, you switch out that allocation so that when you're really old, you have mostly [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] and few [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]].

The idea behind this is that when you're young, you want to take on more risk for more potential growth. And as you age, you don't want as much exposure to volatility and you want more [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] for predictable income. The other reasoning behind it is that as markets crash, usually [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]] are the safe haven. And so when your stock portfolio goes down, your [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bond]] portfolio will go up and that will compensate for some of your losses along the way.

You can rebalance and overall your gains will be smoother and hopefully even better. The problem is as the wise yogi barra once said in theory there is no difference between theory and practice but in practice there is. Now this idea goes along with Harry Marowitz's theory called modern portfolio theory that he developed in the 50s which establishes an efficient frontier with all investing strategies and that basically if you want more returns you have to be willing to accept more risk and there's always a trade-off there and if you don't want to accept the higher levels of risk then you will just have to be okay with accepting lower levels of returns.

Now, many people are not aware that Harry Marowitz actually hated this theory. Within just a few short years of publishing it, he was trying to get people to not accept it, not talk about it, and he was saying, "Hey, I was wrong here." And unfortunately, it still took off. But the reason why he was saying, "Hey, people shouldn't be looking at this and basing their investing decisions off of this," is because it assumed a normal distribution of returns in markets, which doesn't exist.

In life, there are two forms of distributions of outcomes. You can either have a bell curve or normal distribution or you can have a power curve, also known as a parrito distribution. Things like human weight and human height fall into the category of a normal distribution. In other words, most people fall into the middle categories here of being average height or average weight.

Yes, there are some people with lower height or lower weight and some people with higher weight and height, but the vast majority fall in the middle. But there are other things in life that don't follow a normal distribution. Things like wealth, population of cities, the density in galaxies, and many other things across nature follow power law distributions. In other words, a small percentage of the people hold a large percentage of all the wealth and the rest of the wealth is not evenly distributed, but it is distributed among everybody else to a smaller and smaller extent.

The same thing is true of population in cities. A few cities have almost all the population. A few galaxies have almost all the mass and the rest is spread out among a high number of low performers. This is exactly how markets work as well.

Markets do not follow a normal distribution of returns. Both in terms of time periods, in terms of asset classes, and in terms of individual assets, they all follow power law distributions. This is why when you look at stock market indexes like the S&P 500, the vast majority of the overall performance of an index made up by 500 companies comes from only the top 10 companies in the index. This is not a problem of how the system is constructed.

This is the very nature of anything that is not governed by those normal distribution laws just in nature inherently. This is how stuff like this just works. This is not something that can be changed with policy, with laws, with technology or good intentions. You might be able to change which companies get those outsized returns for the entire index, but you can't change that it happens.
