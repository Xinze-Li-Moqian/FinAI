# Watch for the Fed to Bail Out the Bond Market Soon

If there was ever any doubt which markets mattered most to policymakers and to global economies, we know now for certain it is not the stock market. It is in fact the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] market which has been showing signs of extreme stress bordering on breaking recently. Which is why you need to watch out for the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] bailing out the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] market at some point very soon. Jamie Diamond, the CEO of Chase, recently said that he expects a kurfuffle in the US Treasury market that will prompt a [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] intervention.

Now, a kurfuffle in this case means the entire [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bonds|bond]] market breaking, liquidity seizing up, and somebody big about to go bust go out of business. Something like Lehman Brothers. That's what Diamond means by kurfuffle here. He doesn't simply mean, oh, the tenure goes up a little bit and government financing gets a little bit too expensive.

Kurfuffle means something catastrophic that if it's not dealt with could take down the global financial system, otherwise that would not prompt a [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] intervention. He goes on to say here that the expected turmoil is because of all the rules and regulations and that he believes the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] will only step in when they start to panic a little bit. Again, this is not going to be something from Treasury prices falling a little bit. This is going to be something catastrophic.

Something along the lines of September of 2019. You remember when the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] had been allowing their balance sheet to decline and then suddenly they reversed course and called it not [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|QT]]. Well, what has the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] been doing recently? They've been allowing their balance sheet to decline.

When the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] balance sheet goes up, that's called [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]]. They're injecting liquidity. The opposite is true as well. When the balance sheet goes down, they're withdrawing liquidity. This is one of the many factors that has contributed to [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] going from so low to so high over the last couple of years.

And the lack of liquidity in the system is starting to show through with cracks forming, evidenced by the 10-year yield moving from 3.88% all the way up to 4.58% in the matter of days. A big thing happening here is the unwind of the basis trade, which I've talked about in a recent video already. go back and check that out if you haven't seen it yet. But an underlying issue below even that is the liquidity in the plumbing of the financial system.

When financial institutions need to go get cash, they need that cash to be there available to them. In September of 2019, financial institutions went to go get cash and it was not there. They do this primarily through the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|repo market]] overnight. They go to other financial institutions and say, "Hey, I've got some [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]].

Can I have some cash?" Normally that works fine until everybody goes with their collateral. The [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] tries to get cash and there is none. That's why the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] had to step in and provide that liquidity themselves which is why their balance sheet started increasing again.

This is the type of thing that Jaime Diamond is talking about when he says he expects a kurfuffle that will make the Fed start to panic. Now he also said that this is due to all the rules and regulations that financial institutions face today. And part of this is true as well. When financial institutions do something that people don't like, governments come in and say, "You can't do that anymore."

But those financial institutions will then say, "Okay, we need to find ways to make money still. So within the rules that we have set, we're going to find different creative ways to make money." Usually that involves levering up on those things that are still allowed, which causes hidden risk in the system that blows up all at once. Now, this last piece that Jaime Diamond said is something that I've been predicting for a long time, primarily because people like him are talking about trying to make it happen.

He is advocating for bank rule change, including exempting [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] from the supplementary leverage ratio in order to avoid a repeat of the 2020 Treasury market seizure and allow banks to become more active intermediaries in markets. Right now, banks are restricted on how many [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] they can buy. they hit their risk limits because [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] can go down in price which is what happens when yields increase which means if the bank has to sell that treasury they might be selling it at a loss. Now you say, "Wait, [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] are supposed to be risk-f free.

As long as they can hold on to them until maturity, there's no risk, right?" You're absolutely right. But that as long as they can hold on to them until maturity piece is a really big piece because if there's a [[Concepts/Finance/Banking and Credit/Bank Runs|bank run]] and everybody tries to get their deposits back, well, the banks have to sell the assets that they have to free up the cash. And if those assets are worth less than when the bank bought them, then they're out of luck.

And so one restriction that was placed on banks was how many [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] they're allowed to buy in the first place. So making this rule change would absolutely allow banks to buy more [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. But it doesn't solve the issue all by itself. You would also need an explicit or at least an implicit guarantee by the Fed that if something were to happen in terms of a [[Concepts/Finance/Banking and Credit/Bank Runs|bank run]] that the bank could temporarily sell those [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] to the Fed for full price.

So the Fed could hold on to them until maturity or the bank could purchase them back from the Fed at a future point in time. Now, do we have any recent history, any precedent being set for the Fed doing this? Why yes, I'm glad you asked. Over the last couple of years, the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] established something called the bank term funding program specifically in order to allow the [[Concepts/Finance/Banking and Credit/Bank Bailouts|bailout of banks]] like Silicon Valley Bank so that any banks that had [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] that had unrealized losses on them could temporarily sell them to the Fed, buy them back later.

Now, this program has been shuttered for now, but they could always bring it back, which means that [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] could basically be viewed as reserves for banks. And that's exactly what banks and their executives like Diamond are pushing for. And it seems like the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is aware that problems are going to pop up soon that will require their intervention because you're seeing headlines like this where the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has stated they are absolutely ready to stabilize the market if needed and that they are prepared to act with various tools.
