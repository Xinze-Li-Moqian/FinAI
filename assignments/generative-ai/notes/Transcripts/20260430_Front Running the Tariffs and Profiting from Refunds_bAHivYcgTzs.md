# Front Running the Tariffs and Profiting from Refunds

Unfortunately, it looks like this whole [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariff]] thing that's been going on since the beginning of this administration has been one big forced wealth transfer from Main Street to Wall Street, especially the politically connected. And that's because it looked like some people knew all along how this was going to go, and they are profiting massively from it. So first, if you haven't seen by now, the [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariff]] refunds are starting to begin on Monday, and there are some big companies that are going to be getting some big pay days. If you haven't been following along, basically the Supreme Court ruled that no, the president does not have the authority to just blanket lay [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] like this.

That is a power reserved for Congress, which means that all the [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] have been collected since the beginning of last year, now all that has to be returned. So why is this a giant wealth transfer from Main Street to Wall Street? Well, number one, it's because prices went up to compensate for these [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]]. This is something that any economist could have told you, and it's what actually happened.

A company is there to make a [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]]. And so if their costs go up, what are they going to do? They're going to raise their prices for the end product. Shocker. It's like we all collectively accept that there are [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] on things like alcohol and nicotine products in order to persuade people to buy those things less, because we acknowledge that [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] and [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Fiscal Policy|fiscal policy]] incentivizes behavior.

So if you make something artificially more expensive, it's going to decrease activity in it. It's going to decrease [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] for that thing. And so if a business is in the business of making money, which businesses are, they're going to pass those price hikes on to you, which is why those products like alcohol and nicotine products cost more to you. Those companies aren't just going to accept a smaller [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] margin.

You as a customer will always be trying to pay the absolute smallest amount you can for the thing that you want, and the person selling it to you will always be trying to sell it to you at the absolute maximum price they can, and you still buy it. That's how business works. That's how transactions work. The seller wants more, the buyer wants to pay less, and if a transaction happens, it's because they met in the middle somewhere.

Or maybe they didn't meet in the middle, they just agreed on a price. If they don't agree on a price, the transaction doesn't happen. And so for the last year, companies have been charging more, and people have been paying more. And guess what?

People are used to paying those higher prices now. But all of these retailers are now going to be getting refunds. Companies like Walmart are due back over $10 billion. Target is due $2 billion. Nike could get $1 billion. And it's not just the refunds, there's interest being paid back to these companies as well on the money that they were out for that amount of time.

So these companies are going to be getting refunds for the vast majority of all of the money that they paid out, but remember, they raised prices. And so this is actually going to be a windfall for many of these companies. And the best part is, for these companies, they're not going to be lowering their prices from here. You're already used to paying those higher prices.

And so now that their costs are going back down, but you're still used to paying those higher prices, their [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] margins are just going to benefit. So no, these [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] were not paid by other countries, they were paid by you. And no, it did not go to decreasing the deficit, to paying off the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]], didn't go to social security, didn't go to any expenses that the government has, it's all just going back into the corporate [[Concepts/Economics/Microeconomics/Profit|profit]] margins. Now, if you still doubt that you should be an asset owner at this point, and you've been watching [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Fiscal Policy|fiscal policy]] and [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] and political action for the last 5, 10, 15, 20 years, then maybe you just don't want a better life for yourself.

But if you do, you should probably realize by now that being an asset owner will help put you on the right side of this fence. His company is Cantor Fitzgerald, run by his children. Guess what Cantor Fitzgerald has been doing for the last 6 months or so. Cantor Fitzgerald has been running around buying the rights to [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariff]] refunds if they were ever to happen. And they've been buying them for pennies on the dollar.

So what does this mean? How does this work? Cantor Fitzgerald would go to a big company and they'd say, "Hey, you've paid a billion dollars in [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]]. We will pay you $200 million, we'll pay you $300 million right now. That's just cash you get.

What we get in return is if these [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] are overruled and refunds come back, we'll get the refunds instead of you. So you're walking away with a guaranteed $200, million dollars right now, and what we're paying for is the potential upside that these [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] might be overruled and refunded." Guess what Howard Lutnick was also heavily involved in? Designing the [[Concepts/Economics/International Economics/Tariffs|tariffs]] from the start in a way that was extremely expensive, in a way that was very likely to be overturned and overruled by the Supreme Court.

And now Cantor Fitzgerald is going to be receiving a massive windfall as they have the rights to hundreds of millions of dollars in refunds. They probably knew were going to be happening anyway.
