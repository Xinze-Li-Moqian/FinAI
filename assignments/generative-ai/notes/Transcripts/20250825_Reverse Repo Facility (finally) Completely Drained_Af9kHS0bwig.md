# Reverse Repo Facility (finally) Completely Drained

The [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is finally empty. After a $2.5 trillion spike in balances held at the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility at the Fed, the total balances have finally dwindled down basically to zero. Balances will continue to bounce around a little bit. As you can see, as of Wednesday, August 20th, it was about 35 billion.

But zooming out, you can see it's well under where it's been for the last couple of years and back down to normal levels again. And that $2.5 trillion that was in there at the peak in 2022 has now gone back into the financial system. The dollars that have been sitting in this facility have been providing the excess liquidity specifically for the US government's insatiable appetite for borrowing for the last couple of years. Now that this facility is drained, the question is where will the liquidity come from now?

Because the government is not stopping its borrowing. In fact, it's not even slowing down, it's speeding up. As a recap, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] used the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility as a way to absorb the excess cash deposits that flooded the banking system after all the money printing during 2020. Because cash deposits are a liability for banks, they need to go get assets to offset those liabilities.

But the Fed through its rounds of [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] had just bought up all of the assets off the market, which meant that banks were having a very tough time finding collateral. So the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] opened up the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility and said, "Hey banks, park your cash here. We'll give you a nice [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] and in return for that cash, we'll temporarily give you the collateral, the assets that you need." It was a sweet deal for banks because they were getting literally risk-free money from the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] by parking their cash here.

But as [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] rose, [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] rose, and government borrowing kept on increasing, the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] on tea bills slowly but surely got higher than the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that was getting paid in the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility. Which meant starting in 2023, you saw money start to slowly leave the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility because it was finding better deals just lending to the US government. Again, this is all short-term cash. This is stuff like money market funds.

It wants a place where it's going to be easily accessible. I can get the cash out very soon. Going to get the highest [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] possible and the lowest risk of default. And so the US government has been able to borrow essentially as much as it wants as long as it's issuing T bills, very short-term debt because it was just relying on the trillions of dollars that was leaving the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility to get T bills instead.

But now there's nothing left in the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility. We're back to the situation that we were prior to all of the money printing in 2020. The problem is by the end of September, the Treasury is borrowing another trillion dollars. And by the end of the year, they're tacking on another 600 billion on top of that.

And because President Trump has been adamant doing everything he possibly can to try and get the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to lower rates, he's not rolling that debt over or borrowing long-term debt. He has committed to only issuing short-term debt. That way, once [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] do go down, he can roll out that short-term debt over into lower interest, cheaper debt. But where is the $1.5 trillion going to come from for all this short-term debt?

Well, the first place that liquidity might come from is from the Genius Act. One reason why this administration has been so pro crypto is because it wants to ensure that at the foundation of all of that is [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. In other words, dollars being lent to the US government. More dollars being lent to them, the cheaper their borrowing is.

That is the reason for the Genius Act and this administration being so pro crypto. but it's not enough. The second place we can look is a bill that was recently introduced to Congress called the FAR act, the Fiscal Accountability for Interest on Reserves Act. I don't know why they always have to make it a stupid acronym, but basically this bill proposes eliminating the ability of the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to pay interest on [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]] that are held at the Fed.

Right now, banks hold a total of $3.3 trillion in reserves at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. And you can see that prior to 2008, banks did not hold reserves at the Fed. So what in the world changed that caused so many trillions of dollars to just go sit at the Fed? Well, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] got the authorization from Congress to start paying interest on reserves.

So just like the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Repurchase Agreements (Repo)|reverse repo]] facility, if you are a bank sitting on cash, you're like, "Hey, what are we going to do with all these reserves?" give them to the Fed and the Fed pays you interest. Now, the reason this rule was changed in 2008 so the Fed could start paying interest on reserves held there was because of all the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]]. They wanted an easier way to be able to make sure that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]] stayed within the range that they wanted.

Prior to 2008, the way the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] would make sure the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]] stayed in between their target range was strictly through open market operations. And so the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] every day they would be buying and selling government securities. Little mini bouts of [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] and [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Tightening (QT)|QT]] all day long. When they're buying [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] off the open market, that means they are injecting new cash into the market, making it easier, helping [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] stay lower.

And when they are selling government securities, they're taking cash out of the market, making cash more scarce, driving [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] up. So they would conduct open market operations in order to make sure that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] stayed within their target range. But starting in 2008, because there was so much cash injected into the system, they just thought, hey, this is going to be a lot easier to be able to make sure that we can maintain the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] if we can just say, "Hey, we're going to pay interest on reserves.

So if you can't do it amongst yourselves, give us the reserves and we'll pay you." Now, this bill would eliminate the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve's]] ability to pay interest on reserves, which means we've got $3.3 trillion sitting at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] right now that would have to go look elsewhere for a return on its money. And yes, basically all of that would go into T bills and [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. Now, if you're wondering how the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] would be able to maintain the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|Federal Funds rate]] without paying interest on overnight [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Bank Reserves|reserve balances]] at the Fed, they would just go back to open market operations.

In other words, manipulating their balance sheet in order to change the reserves in the system to target the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] range. It is possible that reserves right now are still way higher than they would need to be for the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to be able to do this easily. But either way, if this bill gets passed, they amend the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]]. So the Fed stops paying interest on reserves. $3 trillion floods out of the Fed, goes buys tea bills, go buy [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], goes and does other stuff.

Lots of easing, lots of new money being put to work in the economy. Now, the next and most likely option in my opinion is this is the Fed's balance sheet. We just see them return to [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] and this thing starts to grow again. If the government keeps on borrowing more and more, driving [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] high, we get somebody else in charge at the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]], they just start up [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] again, they become the lender to the US government at the rate the government wants.

I think this is more likely because it's much easier. The Fed's been doing [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] for like two decades now. It would not require a huge change to the banking system or the way the Fed operates. It wouldn't require financial institutions to get used to the Fed doing open market operations and the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] being more volatile.

and it would accomplish the same goal. The government needs somebody to borrow from. They can borrow from the Fed. I think this is the most likely, but probably not under the current leadership at the Fed. Now, obviously, even this is just a short-term band-aid because [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]] doesn't necessarily mean [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] stay lower.

[[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|Quantitative easing]] is where the Fed says, "We'll buy a certain number of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]], but a lot of other people could be selling those [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Bonds|bonds]]." Which means that even if [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] is happening, you could see something like the 10-year yield still go higher because there's more selling than there is buying. Which means that the long game here with so much [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] in T bills right now, I think [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control is likely the long-term solution they'll use. [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|Yield curve]] control is like [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] but on steroids.

It's where the Fed says, "We don't care how many, let's say, 10-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]] we have to buy. We're going to buy as many of them as necessary to make sure the yield stays at 4% or 3% or 2% or whatever they choose. Which means the rest of the world could sell an unlimited amount of them and the Fed would just buy them all at that price. in order for the US government to be able to successfully roll over the trillions of dollars of debt that it has in short-term debt right now at an [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] that is lower than the current market rate.

I think [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control is more likely than not, especially considering the fact they did this in the 40s and the 50s last time we were here. Especially considering the fact that the next year or two we're going to see higher prices and the market reaction is going to be push [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] higher. So, as the Fed tries to lower rates under pressure from Trump, it's only going to push down the low end of the curve, not going to do anything to help them roll over all the debt into longer term debt, which means balance sheet probably goes to the moon.
