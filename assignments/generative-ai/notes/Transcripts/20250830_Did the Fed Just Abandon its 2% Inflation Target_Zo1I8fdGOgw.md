# Did the Fed Just Abandon its 2% Inflation Target?

In fact, that's what's happened for all of human history is [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Deflation|deflation]].

It means you get more for less. It means all the progress and innovation and invention and efficiency shows up in more abundance of goods and wealth and things that people want. which means it is cheaper, less cost to obtain the things people want. So even [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] that causes prices to stay the same is still theft of the cheaper cost of living people would have otherwise.

However, beyond that, it's somehow just accepted by everyone that prices going up by 2% every year is somehow considered stable. But now you see people talking about Powell saying that the Fed has adopted a new framework that removes a reference to the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] seeking [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] that averages 2% over time saying that the Fed is deleting their 2% target that only judgment remains and longerterm [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]] well anchored at 2% is a good thing. And Victory Laps saying years ago I predicted the Fed would increase their [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] target and now they did something even crazier.

They removed any [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] target at all. Unfortunately for the doomers, but fortunately for the rest of us, this is not what happened here. In Powell's speech at Jacksonhole, he unveiled a new framework to approach [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]]. And the new framework removes some of the language that more narrowly focused on the preandemic challenge of persistently low [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]].

In other words, they are abandoning the average [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] target AIT framework. That was a policy that allowed [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] to exceed 2% temporarily in order to offset prior undershoots. Okay, so in plain language, what does this mean? Prior to 2020, the [[Concepts/Finance/Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] targeted 2% [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]].

When [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] was lower than they wanted, they would try and do things to get [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] higher. While [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] was higher than they wanted, they would try and do things to get [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] lower. But after the trillions of dollars that they printed out of nothing starting in 2020, [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] shockingly shot through the roof. Prices were going up like crazy.

So they unveiled at that time a new framework and said, "Hey, it's okay that [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] is higher than normal right now because we're looking at things through a new framework called average [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] targeting." which means yes [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] is higher right now but we don't need to care about that because it was lower earlier. So on average over the course of however many years we want to look at were still golden and because of that new framework of AIT average [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] targeting they waited way too long to try and undo their mistakes from all the money printing.

If [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]] is too low, we're going to do about something about it right now.
