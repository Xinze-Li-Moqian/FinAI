# Did the Fed Just Abandon its 2% Inflation Target?

Did the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] just abandon its 2% [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target? It's well known that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is tasked by Congress with price stability. And yet, for some reason, everybody has accepted for decades now that it's okay. The [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] interprets price stability as 2% [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]].

Now, to me, prices going up mean that prices aren't stable. Stable prices mean prices aren't changing. But guess what? Even that would still be theft because [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Deflation|deflation]] is what would happen naturally over time without intervention monetarily. In fact, that's what's happened for all of human history is [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Deflation|deflation]].

It means you get more for less. It means all the progress and innovation and invention and efficiency shows up in more abundance of goods and wealth and things that people want. which means it is cheaper, less cost to obtain the things people want. So even [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Monetary Policy|monetary policy]] that causes prices to stay the same is still theft of the cheaper cost of living people would have otherwise.

However, beyond that, it's somehow just accepted by everyone that prices going up by 2% every year is somehow considered stable. But now you see people talking about Powell saying that the Fed has adopted a new framework that removes a reference to the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] seeking [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] that averages 2% over time saying that the Fed is deleting their 2% target that only judgment remains and longerterm [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation Expectations|inflation expectations]] well anchored at 2% is a good thing. And Victory Laps saying years ago I predicted the Fed would increase their [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target and now they did something even crazier.

They removed any [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target at all. Unfortunately for the doomers, but fortunately for the rest of us, this is not what happened here. In Powell's speech at Jacksonhole, he unveiled a new framework to approach [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. And the new framework removes some of the language that more narrowly focused on the preandemic challenge of persistently low [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]].

In other words, they are abandoning the average [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target AIT framework. That was a policy that allowed [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] to exceed 2% temporarily in order to offset prior undershoots. Okay, so in plain language, what does this mean? Prior to 2020, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] targeted 2% [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]].

When [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] was lower than they wanted, they would try and do things to get [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] higher. While [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] was higher than they wanted, they would try and do things to get [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] lower. But after the trillions of dollars that they printed out of nothing starting in 2020, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] shockingly shot through the roof. Prices were going up like crazy.

So they unveiled at that time a new framework and said, "Hey, it's okay that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is higher than normal right now because we're looking at things through a new framework called average [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] targeting." which means yes [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is higher right now but we don't need to care about that because it was lower earlier. So on average over the course of however many years we want to look at were still golden and because of that new framework of AIT average [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] targeting they waited way too long to try and undo their mistakes from all the money printing.

And so for the last couple of months, the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] has been reviewing this average [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] targeting framework that they've been making decisions through and saying, "Hey, we're getting rid of that. We're going back to the way things were before where if [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is too high, we're going to do something about it right now. If [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] is too low, we're going to do about something about it right now. And we're not going to worry about what the average has been over a period of time."

Now, to be honest, I am not sure what [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] numbers they were looking at when [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] was all the way up at 8 or 9% by mid 2022 because prior to all of the money printing, [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] barely ever dipped below 2%. There's a brief period of time where it dipped down to zero and it shot right back up. So even given their average [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target framework, I'm not exactly sure what they were using to offset the record high [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] that came afterwards that justified their delayed action. But still, it's a good thing that they are abandoning that framework because they're just going to be targeting the current [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] rate now.

They're not abandoning the 2% target. They're abandoning the average 2% [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] target. This is a chart of [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], specifically the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Policy Interest Rates|federal funds rate]] that the Fed sets. And you can see they started cutting rates in 2024 from an effective rate of 5.3% down to 4.3%.

Many people are really excited for the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] to start cutting rates again soon. the president especially being one of them because he's sitting on $10 trillion of [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Sovereign Debt|government debt]] that is being rolled over every single year into short-term T bills that he wants to refinance roll over into longerterm debt once it's cheaper. So he wants the Fed to cut rates so he can lock in those lower debt prices. But the problem is but the problem is the last time the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] cut rates in 2024, it took the 10-year Treasury from 3.6% all the way up to 4.8 8%.

This was the opposite effect that it had on things like tea bills. Short-term debt, the [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] did go down along with the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] cuts. But long-term debt, like even 30-year [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]], went from 3.9% all the way up to 5%. Which means that while the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is likely to start cutting rates soon, it is not likely to have a significant impact on long-term [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] going lower.

This would affect things like [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]], [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]], and even credit card debt. And the reason is because of this chart right here, the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. You know, the thing that the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is supposed to now keep at 2% because the market understands that if the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] lowers rates, it makes short-term borrowing for the US government easier. When the government spends that money into existence, it drives the money supply higher.

When there's more money, that means higher prices, more [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]]. When there's higher prices and more [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]], it means debt in the free market or at least somewhat controlled by the free market like longerterm [[Finance/03 Government Debt and Fiscal Policy/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]] and [[Finance/09 Housing and Mortgages/Mortgages|mortgages]] and [[Finance/14 Personal Finance and Retirement/Auto Loans|auto loans]]. It means [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] of all that debt will go up in order to compensate for that extra loss in [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]]. This means the [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Federal Reserve|Federal Reserve]] is about to make its job even harder by lighting [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] on fire from cutting [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]], which will at the same time make long-term US government borrowing more expensive.

The only logical next step after this happens is going to be a return to [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|quantitative easing]]. And if that's not enough, then even outright [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control, which they have talked about. In fact, Scott Bessant, the Treasury Secretary, specifically said that they want a lower 10-year yield, not just Fed cuts. The only way to accomplish lower yields, the long end of the curve, if Fed cuts don't do it, is with [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Quantitative Easing (QE)|QE]] and or [[Finance/04 Bonds and Funding Markets/Yield Curves|yield curve]] control.
