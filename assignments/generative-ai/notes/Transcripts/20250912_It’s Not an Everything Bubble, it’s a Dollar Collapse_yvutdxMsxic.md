# It’s Not an Everything Bubble, it’s a Dollar Collapse

Everywhere you look today, prices are rising quickly. Whether you're looking at assets or goods, real estate or [[Finance/10 Markets and Portfolios/Stocks|stocks]], crypto or gold, it looks like everything is a bubble. But if everything is in a bubble, maybe nothing is. Maybe the real problem is in your measuring stick.

Maybe the real problem lies with your denominator, your currency, the dollar. The price of gold has recently broken out from a multi-month consolidation pattern and is currently at all-time highs, trading for $3,629 per ounce. [[Finance/08 Gold Silver and Commodities/Silver|Silver]] as well is hitting prices that it has only seen a few times in history at over $41 per ounce. And the United States Case Schiller home price index is also at all-time highs. So we have hard assets like gold and [[Finance/08 Gold Silver and Commodities/Silver|silver]]. even real estate. So, it's not just assets. It is the cost of life in general that is all going up and nothing has ever been this expensive. Historically, when there is a bubble, it is an outlier. Which means this chart looks pretty much exactly like what you should expect because if every government and [[Finance/01 Central Banking/Central Banks|central bank]] around the world is devaluing their own currency at around approximately the same rate, of course you're not going to have that large of fluctuations in the value of one currency compared to another, which is why the DXY doesn't look anything crazy. Budwe von Misus was one of the fathers of Austrian economics and he described something called a crackup boom. He said if once public opinion is convinced that the increase in the quantity of money will continue and never come to an end and that consequently the prices of all commodities and services will not cease to rise.

Everybody becomes eager to buy as much as possible and to restrict his cash holding to a minimum size. For under these circumstances, the regular costs incurred by holding cash are increased by the losses caused by the progressive fall in [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]]. The advantages of holding cash must be paid for by sacrifices which are deemed unreasonably burdensome. This phenomenon was in the great European inflations of the 20s called flight into real goods or crackup boom.

In other words, any rational person will choose to get rid of dollars as fast and as soon as possible if they know the [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] of those dollars is going to continue declining. People will buy anything and everything they can because at least they're preserving their [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Purchasing Power|purchasing power]] better than if they hold on to those dollars. While we are not experiencing anything closely related to what could be called [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Hyperinflation|hyperinflation]], this is the phenomenon that we are seeing happen in America today. Those who have the means to do so are buying assets to protect their money and those who do not have the means to do so are dealing with the consequences of that [[Finance/02 Inflation and Purchasing Power/Inflation|inflation]] by buying goods.
