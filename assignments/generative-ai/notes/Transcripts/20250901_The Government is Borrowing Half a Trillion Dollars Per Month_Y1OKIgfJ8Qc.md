# The Government is Borrowing Half a Trillion Dollars Per Month

The US government has added $1 trillion to the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] in the last 2 months alone. For anybody who hears the word trillions these days and is just numb to it because it seems like everything is trillions, let me clarify. The United States government has borrowed an additional $500 billion per month for the last two months straight. This table comes straight from the Treasury itself.

website is fiscalata.treasury.gov and you can see that at the end of June the total [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] was $ 36.2 trillion. We know that since the US government does not produce anything, it has to take what it wants to spend through [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]], but it wants to spend even more than it can take in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]]. So, whatever they want to spend past what they take in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]], they have to borrow from willing lenders. And just one month later, by the end of July, the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] was up to $36.9 trillion.

Going back to the end of June, we see that 36.2 trillion to the end of July at 36.9 trillion is an increase of about $700 billion just to the debt. which means everything they took in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] they spent and then they borrowed an additional 700 billion just in one month. And then from the end of July at 36.9 trillion to the end of August at 37.3 trillion, that was an additional $400 billion of borrowing just in the month of August. Which means in a two-month time period, not only did the US government take all the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] that it wanted to take, spent all that money, but on top of that, borrowed an additional $1.1 trillion.

Now, seeing how fast the US debt is growing right now because of all of the excess spending and borrowing again is hard to put into perspective, but at this current run rate, that would mean the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] would be increasing annually by $6 trillion. Keep in mind, in 2024, the US government spent a total of $6.9 trillion. Yet they took a total of $4.9 trillion in [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]], which meant that in 2024, the total deficit was only 2 trillion. Now, I say only two trillion.

You can clearly see by this chart that that is trending in the wrong direction. It's been trending in the wrong direction forever. And $2 trillion as just a deficit is absolutely wild. But at the current run rate over the last two months, the deficit is on pace for an annual $6 trillion deficit, not spending, not [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Budget Deficits|budget deficit]].

The amount that is borrowed in excess of [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]]. Even the record in 2020 was only a $3.3 trillion deficit. Again, the deficit is the amount that's added to the [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Sovereign Debt|national debt]] every year. And last year, there was a $2 trillion deficit total. While in the last 2 months alone, the US government has borrowed a full $1.1 trillion in 2 months.

Okay, I know I've beat that horse to death by now, but it's not as bad as you think because you're probably sitting there thinking, where in the world is all this money going? And the answer is a lot of it is going into the Treasury General Account. The Treasury General Account is basically the US government's overall checking account. So when the government takes money with [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Taxation|taxes]] and they borrow money, all that cash goes into the Treasury general account and then anytime they spend money on anything to anybody, whether it's politicians paychecks, whether it is government contractors, whether it is social security checks, it comes out of this account.

You can see that in mid July, the Treasury General account was sitting at around $296 billion. By the end of August, this account was up to $589 billion, which means about $300 billion has gone into the government's checking account. So, yes, they are borrowing a lot right now. $1.1 trillion over just the last 2 months, but at least $300 billion of that is still just sitting in their checking account. And the Treasury announced in their most recent statement their plans through the end of the year is to get this balance up to 850 billion.

And they plan to do that by the end of September, which means that within the month of September, so one more month as of the time of this recording, this Treasury General account will be up to $850 billion, which means they have another $260 billion or so to borrow just to stash away into this account. Explained very simply, imagine you're sitting there with an annual income of $100,000, but every year you spend $200,000. And you do that by putting everything on a credit card. One day you look at your checking account and realize your checking account is down to about, I don't know, $2,000.

You think to yourself, "Ah, I'd really like my checking account to be $8,000." So, you just take a cash advance on your credit card that you're already spending an extra hundred grand on per year, and you use that cash advance to put into your checking account just because you like that buffer zone. Sound ridiculous? Well, that is literally exactly what the US government is doing right now.

Now, if you take a look at a longerterm view of the Treasury General account, you can see that periodically this account does get drawn down very close to zero. And that's because of [[Concepts/Economics/Public Economics/Debt Ceilings|debt ceiling]] limit negotiations and government shutdowns and when they have to engage in emergency measures. Usually, anything in this account does get drawn down. And so for the last couple of years, the Treasury has liked to keep this account full around $850 billion so that when they have to use this for spending, they have it in there.

Now, if you're wondering who in their right mind is doing all of this lending to the US government, well, quite a few people actually because if you take a look at yields on short-term debt, this is T bills, they've actually been going down, which means there is more demand than supply. This is largely going to be cash in things like money market funds, high yield savings accounts, corporate [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|treasuries]]. This is all highly liquid cash that wants to have a return that is both safe and at least close to the rate of [[Concepts/Economics/Macroeconomics/Inflation|inflation]].

And even with longerterm debt like 10-year [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Treasury Securities and Issuance|Treasuries]], if those [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts do indeed make their way over to the longer end of the curve means there could be an opportunity to make gains on the [[Concepts/Finance/Markets and Instruments/Bond Prices and Yields|bond prices]] themselves. Moving higher. Now, if you've ever watched my videos before, you know that I do not think the Fed's [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rate]] cuts that are coming up soon are going to hit the long end of the curve.

They will make government borrowing cheaper, but only for things like tea bills, only the short end of the curve. And while the US government has been doing all their borrowing at the short end of the curve, with the express purpose of waiting till [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] go down so they can roll over that debt and secure longerterm, lower, cheaper [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]]. This has pushed the total US money supply up to a brand new record of over $22 trillion, surpassing its peak in 2022. Now, what you'll notice is if you look at the growth rate of the money supply going all the way back to 1960, the typical range for the growth rate is between 5 and 10%.

We will be there very soon. Especially with [[Concepts/Economics/Monetary Economics/Interest and Interest Rates|interest rates]] about to decline because in the modern-day digital fiat financial system that we have, dollars are loaned into existence.
